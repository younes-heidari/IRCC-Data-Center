\section{System Description}

This report covers the heat exchanger selection and pressure-drop modelling for
\textbf{IRCC 2026, Project~\#3}: a 150\,kW R-290 water-cooled chiller designed for a
high-efficiency edge-compute data centre facility near Champaign, IL.

The system uses a vapour-compression cycle with propane (R-290) as the working fluid.
Heat is absorbed from the chilled-water loop in the evaporator, compressed, and rejected
to an intermediate 30\,\% propylene-glycol (PG) loop in the condenser. The glycol loop
then carries the heat to an outdoor dry cooler with variable-speed fans. A waterside
economiser (glycol-to-chilled-water plate HX) allows free cooling when outdoor
temperatures are low enough to bypass the compressor entirely.

\midrule

Minimum cooling load & 30\,kW (5:1 turndown) & Project spec \\
Chilled-water supply\,/\,return & 15\,/\,21\,\textdegree C & ASHRAE TC 9.9 W17 \\
Refrigerant & R-290 (propane), ASHRAE A3, GWP $< 150$ & US EPA AIM Act \\

\section{Scope of This Report}

This document covers the two refrigerant-side plate heat exchangers:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:evap} --- Evaporator BPHE}: R-290 evaporates against
        chilled water (21\,\textdegree C\,$\to$\,15\,\textdegree C). Thermal sizing by
        LMTD; selection from the Kelvion DW series; refrigerant-side pressure-drop model
        using the Han, Lee \& Kim (2003) \emph{evaporation} correlation as implemented
        in \texttt{evaporator.py}.
  \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:cond} --- Condenser BPHE}: R-290 condensing rejects
        heat to the 30\,\% propylene-glycol loop (40\,\textdegree C\,$\to$\,46\,\textdegree C).
        Thermal sizing by counter-flow LMTD (three-zone approximation); same Kelvion DW
        model family; pressure-drop model using the Han, Lee \& Kim (2003)
        \emph{condensation} correlation as implemented in \texttt{condenser.py}.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Both chapters follow the same document structure:
\textbf{Part~A} (sizing and model selection) followed by

All geometry parameters marked \assumed{} are placeholders pending a model-specific vendor

% ── §1b.1 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Project Constraints}
\label{refrig:sec:constraints}

The project brief fixes two hard constraints on refrigerant selection before any
performance comparison is meaningful:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GWP $< 150$} (US EPA AIM Act Technology Transitions, \S40 CFR Part 84).
  \item \textbf{ASHRAE 34 safety classification A1, A2L, or A3 only.}
\end{itemize}

These two constraints alone eliminate several common candidates without needing a
cycle calculation at all:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{R-32} (GWP $\approx$675) and \textbf{R-454B} (GWP $\approx$466) --- both
        popular low(er)-GWP HFCs, but both fail the project's GWP$<$150 requirement
        outright.
  \item \textbf{Ammonia (R-717)} --- GWP 0, excellent thermodynamic performance, but
        ASHRAE safety class \textbf{B2L} (toxic). The project brief's safety-class list
        (\S\ref{refrig:sec:constraints}) explicitly admits only A1/A2L/A3; B-class
        (toxic) fluids are out of scope regardless of GWP or COP.
  \item \textbf{CO$_2$ (R-744)} --- GWP 1, ASHRAE class A1 (non-toxic, non-flammable),
        satisfies both hard constraints. It is nonetheless excluded here on a third,
        cycle-architecture ground: R-744's critical temperature is only
        31.1\,\textdegree C, well below this project's 52\,\textdegree C design
        condensing temperature (Chapter~\ref{ch:cond}, \S\ref{cond:sec:subcool}). A
        standard subcritical cycle --- the architecture every other chapter of this
        report is built around --- is not physically possible for CO$_2$ at this
        condensing condition; it would require a transcritical cycle (gas cooler
        instead of a condenser, a throttle/expander sized for supercritical inlet
        conditions, and a materially different high-side pressure regime,
        $\sim$90\,bar rather than $\sim$18\,bar). This is a legitimate refrigerant in
        other applications but a different design exercise from the one this report
        undertakes, so it is not carried into the performance comparison below.
\end{itemize}

% ── §1b.3 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Comparison Method}
\label{refrig:sec:method}

The detailed compressor model used elsewhere in this report (Chapter~\ref{ch:comp})
is a vendor-published AHRI-540/EN12900 performance map, which exists only for the
specific refrigerant/compressor pair actually selected (R-290, Bitzer 4FEP-35Z) ---
no equivalent map exists yet for the other two candidates, since no compressor has
been selected for them. Comparing refrigerants on that basis would really be
comparing compressors, not working fluids.

Instead, all three candidates are evaluated through the \textbf{same idealized
single-stage cycle}, with every non-refrigerant-dependent parameter held identical:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Evaporating temperature 7\,\textdegree C, condensing temperature
        52\,\textdegree C --- this project's own design point (Chapter~\ref{ch:cond}).
  \item 8\,K superheat, 5\,K subcooling --- matching this project's design assumptions.
  \item A single fixed isentropic compressor efficiency, $\eta_{\mathrm{isentropic}} =
        0.70$, applied identically to all three candidates.
\end{itemize}

Holding every parameter fixed except the refrigerant itself isolates the working
fluid's own thermodynamic contribution to COP from any compressor-specific
efficiency curve --- appropriate for a screening-level comparison made \emph{before}
a compressor is selected, which is exactly the point in the design process
Chapter~\ref{ch:comp}'s real vendor map (developed only after R-290 was already
chosen) does not yet inform. Implemented in \texttt{refrigerant\_comparison.py}.

\subsection{Interpreting the Result: R-1234ze(E)'s Higher COP}
\label{refrig:sec:interpret}

R-1234ze(E) shows the \emph{highest} idealized COP of the three candidates (3.54 vs.\
R-290's 3.45, a $\approx$2.4\,\% margin) --- yet R-290 is the refrigerant carried
forward through the rest of this report. This is not an inconsistency; it reflects a
second engineering axis the COP number alone does not capture: \textbf{volumetric
refrigerating effect} (refrigerating capacity per unit of compressor swept volume).
R-290 delivers $\approx$3{,}270\,kJ/m$^3$ against R-1234ze(E)'s
$\approx$1{,}880\,kJ/m$^3$ --- R-290 moves roughly 1.7$\times$ more cooling duty
through the same size compressor. This traces directly back to R-1234ze(E)'s lower
suction pressure at this evaporating temperature (2.78\,bar(a) vs.\ R-290's
5.84\,bar(a)): a lower-density suction gas needs proportionally more swept volume to
move the same mass flow. R-1234yf sits between the other two on suction pressure
(3.98\,bar(a)) but trails both on COP (3.38), so it does not improve on R-290 on
either axis.

Practically, this means an R-1234ze(E)-based system sized for the same 150\,kW duty
would need a meaningfully larger (and more expensive) compressor than the Bitzer
4FEP-35Z pair selected in Chapter~\ref{ch:comp}, for only a marginal COP gain. Both
A2L candidates would also forgo the mature hydrocarbon-refrigerant compressor,
oil-separator (Chapter~\ref{ch:oilsep}), and receiver (Chapter~\ref{ch:receiver})
ecosystem this report's Bitzer selections already draw on --- a real switching cost
that a $\approx$2\,\% idealized COP gain does not obviously outweigh.

% ── §1b.5 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Selection: R-290 (Propane)}
\label{refrig:sec:selection}

R-290 is selected as the project's working fluid on the combination of:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Satisfies both hard constraints (GWP 3, ASHRAE class A3) with large margin
        against the GWP$<$150 threshold.
  \item Competitive idealized COP (3.45, within 2.4\,\% of the best-performing
        candidate) \emph{and} the best volumetric refrigerating effect of the three
        candidates, favouring a compact, cost-effective compressor selection
        (\S\ref{refrig:sec:interpret}).
  \item A mature, single-vendor hydrocarbon-refrigerant equipment ecosystem was
        available for every pressure-vessel and accessory this design needs: the
        Bitzer 4FEP-35Z compressor (Chapter~\ref{ch:comp}), OA-series oil separator
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:oilsep}), and P-series liquid receiver
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:receiver}) are all explicitly rated for A3 hydrocarbon
        refrigerants from a single manufacturer --- simplifying procurement and
        spare-parts management, the same rationale already applied to the BPHE
        vendor selections (Chapters~\ref{ch:evap}, \ref{ch:cond}).
  \item R-290's A3 flammability is the trade-off accepted for the above: it drives
        the double-wall BPHE construction (Chapters~\ref{ch:evap}, \ref{ch:cond}),
        the oil separator's float-valve (not solenoid) oil return
        (\S\ref{oilsep:sec:oilreturn}), and the liquid receiver's
        charge-minimization sizing (\S\ref{receiver:sec:safety}) --- each already
        flagged and addressed in its own chapter rather than treated as a
        blanket risk.
\end{enumerate}

% ── §1b.6 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Limitations and Open Items}
\label{refrig:sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Fixed 70\,\% isentropic efficiency is a simplifying assumption},
        applied identically across all three candidates specifically so the
        comparison isolates refrigerant thermodynamics rather than a compressor
        efficiency curve (\S\ref{refrig:sec:method}). Real compressor efficiency
        varies by refrigerant, displacement, and operating point; R-290's real
        selected-compressor performance (Chapter~\ref{ch:comp}) should be treated as
        the authoritative number for this project, not the idealized value here.
  \item \textbf{No charge/leak-risk quantification is performed for the A2L or A3
        candidates in this chapter} beyond the safety-class screen itself
        (\S\ref{refrig:sec:constraints}); the detailed charge estimate
        (\S\ref{receiver:sec:charge}) and A3-specific mitigations are developed only
        for the selected fluid (R-290) in their respective downstream chapters.
  \item \textbf{GWP values are approximate}, drawn from commonly cited AR5 (IPCC
        2013), 100-year integrated time horizon figures per ASHRAE Standard 34 GWP
        tables; exact values vary slightly by source/assessment report vintage and
        should be confirmed against the specific regulatory basis (e.g., AIM Act
        Technology Transitions Table) before final compliance sign-off.
  \item \textbf{R-1234yf and R-1234ze(E) are evaluated as pure single-component
        fluids} via CoolProp; several commercial A2L blends (e.g., R-454B, R-455A)
        exist but were not screened here, either because they fail the GWP$<$150
        constraint outright (R-454B) or were judged unlikely to outperform the two
        pure HFOs already included on this comparison's own metrics.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Design Basis}

The evaporator duty and water-side mass flow rate are summarised below.
The water flow rate follows directly from the specified capacity and temperature drop:

\[
  \mdot = \frac{Q}{c_p\,\Delta T}
        = \frac{150{,}000\;\mathrm{W}}{4{,}186\;\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}} \times 6\;\mathrm{K}}
        = 5.97\;\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}
        \approx 21.5\;\mathrm{m^3\,h^{-1}}
        \approx 95\;\text{US\,GPM}
\]

% ── §2.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Thermal Sizing --- LMTD Method}

Because the refrigerant boils at constant saturation temperature, the refrigerant side is
isothermal and the LMTD formula reduces to:

\[
  \Tlm = \frac{\Delta T_1 - \Delta T_2}{\ln\!\left(\Delta T_1 / \Delta T_2\right)}
\]

\begin{table}[h!]
\caption{Terminal temperature differences.}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Terminal} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
$\Delta T_1$ & $T_{\mathrm{water,in}} - T_{\mathrm{sat}} = 21 - 7$ & 14\,\textdegree C \\
$\Delta T_2$ & $T_{\mathrm{water,out}} - T_{\mathrm{sat}} = 15 - 7$ & 8\,\textdegree C \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\[
  \Tlm = \frac{14 - 8}{\ln(14/8)} = \frac{6}{0.5596} = 10.72\;\text{\textdegree C}
\]

\[
  A = \frac{Q}{U\,\Tlm} = \frac{150{,}000}{3{,}000 \times 10.72} = 4.66\;\mathrm{m^2}
\]

Adding $\approx$20\,\% margin for subcooled and superheated zones gives a practical
target of approximately 5.5--6.0\,m$^2$.

\subsection{Reasonableness of $U = 3{,}000\;\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-1}}$}

For a BPHE evaporator (water one side, propane boiling on the other), published values of
$U$ typically fall in the 2{,}500--4{,}500\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ range. Propane's favourable
transport properties (low viscosity, good wetting) push achievable $U$ toward the
upper-middle of that range, so 3{,}000\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ is a reasonable, slightly
conservative preliminary assumption.

% ── §2.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Design Pressure Determination}

Although the evaporator operates at $\approx$5\,bar(g), the design pressure is governed
by the \emph{standstill condition}: with no flow, refrigerant equalises to the highest
ambient temperature the system may reach. Using 55\,\textdegree C (ASHRAE~15 / EN~378 /
IEC~60335-2-40), propane saturation pressure is $\approx$19.1\,bar(a) / 18.1\,bar(g).
The BPHE design pressure must exceed 18\,bar(g); standard ratings of 30--31\,bar or
45\,bar comfortably satisfy this requirement.

% ── §2.4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Heat Exchanger Technology and Safety Considerations}

\subsection{Flammable Refrigerant (A3) and Occupied-Space Considerations}

Propane is classified as an A3 (flammable) refrigerant. Where a BPHE separates an A3
refrigerant from chilled water circulating into a data centre, codes such as EN~378,
ASHRAE~15, and IEC~60335-2-89 typically require double-wall / leak-safe construction or
strict charge limits. A double-wall (``DW'') construction is adopted for this design
pending final confirmation of the applicable code requirement.

% ── §2.5 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Candidate Screening --- Kelvion Brazed Plate Heat Exchanger Range}

The Kelvion \textbf{GB-DW series} (45\,bar, double-wall) satisfies both the standstill
pressure requirement and the A3 safety requirement. The \textbf{GBH-DW~500H} is the
leading candidate.

\section{Source Correlation: Han, Lee \& Kim (2003) --- Evaporation}

\subsection{Experimental Basis}

The correlation covers R410A and R22 evaporating inside BPHEs with chevron angles
20\textdegree, 35\textdegree, and 45\textdegree. Test conditions:

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hydraulic Diameter}

\begin{equation}
  \Dh = \frac{4 \times \text{(channel flow area)}}{\text{(wetted surface)}} = \frac{2b}{\phi},
  \qquad \phi = 1.17
  \tag{Eq.\,4}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Quality and Equivalent Flow Parameters}

\begin{equation}
  \Delta x = x_{\mathrm{out}} - x_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{Q_w}{\mdot_r\,i_{fg}}
  \tag{Eq.\,7}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  G_{\mathrm{Eq}} = G_c\!\left[(1-x) + x\left(\frac{\rho_f}{\rho_g}\right)^{\!1/2}\right],
  \qquad G_c = \frac{\mdot}{N_{cp}\,b\,L_w}
  \tag{Eq.\,11}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  Re_{\mathrm{Eq}} = \frac{G_{\mathrm{Eq}}\,\Dh}{\mu_f}
  \tag{Eq.\,20}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Two-Phase Friction Factor Correlation}

\begin{align}
  f    &= Ge_3 \cdot Re_{\mathrm{Eq}}^{\,Ge_4} \tag{Eq.\,17} \\[6pt]
  Ge_3 &= 64{,}710 \times \!\left(\frac{p_{co}}{\Dh}\right)^{\!-5.27}
           \!\times\! \left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta\right)^{\!-3.03} \tag{Eq.\,18} \\[6pt]
  Ge_4 &= -1.314 \times \!\left(\frac{p_{co}}{\Dh}\right)^{\!-0.62}
           \!\times\! \left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta\right)^{\!-0.47} \tag{Eq.\,19}
\end{align}

\subsection{Frictional Pressure Drop}

\begin{equation}
  \Delta P_{\mathrm{fr}} = f \times \frac{L_v\,N_{cp}}{\Dh}
                           \times \frac{G_{\mathrm{Eq}}^2}{\rho_f}
  \tag{Eq.\,12}
\end{equation}

Acceleration, static-head, and port losses account for less than 5\,\% of the total;
only the frictional term is modelled.

\subsection{Validity Ranges}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\beta$: 20\textdegree--45\textdegree; $G$: 13--34\,kg\,m$^{-2}$s$^{-1}$;
        $x$: 0.15--0.95; evaporation temperature: 5--15\,\textdegree C.
  \item Fluids tested: R410A and R22 --- not R290. Transfer to propane is treated as
        reasonable by practitioners but has not been independently validated
        (Open Item 14.2.5).
\end{itemize}

% ── §2.10 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Implementation in \texttt{evaporator.py}}

\subsection{Two-Zone Structure}

The model splits the refrigerant path into a \textbf{boiling zone}
($x_{\mathrm{in}} \to 1$, Han two-phase correlation) and a \textbf{superheat zone}
(saturated vapour $\to$ outlet, Blasius single-phase). Zone lengths are apportioned
by enthalpy:

\[
  L_{\mathrm{boiling}} = L \times \frac{h_g - h_{\mathrm{in}}}{h_{\mathrm{out}} - h_{\mathrm{in}}}
\]

\subsection{Boiling-Zone Integration}

The local pressure gradient is integrated across 200 quality steps from $x_{\mathrm{in}}$
to~1 using \texttt{np.trapezoid}, capturing the continuous variation of $G_{\mathrm{Eq}}$,
$Re_{\mathrm{Eq}}$, and $f$ with quality.

\subsection{Superheat-Zone Treatment}

A smooth-channel Blasius friction factor is used ($16/Re$ for laminar,
$0.316\,Re^{-0.25}$ for turbulent). No reference to $N_{cp}$ is needed in this zone ---
the per-channel flux $G$ already accounts for channel count via $A_{\mathrm{flow}}$.

% ── §2.13 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Water-Side Pressure Drop: Martin (1996) Correlation}
\label{evap:sec:martin}

\subsection{Why a Second Correlation Is Needed}

The Han et al.\ (2003) correlation used in \S\ref{evap:sec:dh}--\ref{evap:sec:chspacing}
is a \emph{two-phase} friction factor formulated around the equivalent mass flux
$G_{\mathrm{Eq}}$, which blends liquid and vapour density across vapour quality. The
chilled-water stream never changes phase, so $G_{\mathrm{Eq}}$ is undefined there and
Han's correlation cannot be applied. The single-phase chevron friction correlation of
\textbf{Martin (1996)} --- already adopted for the economizer's two liquid streams
(\S\ref{econ:sec:martin}) --- is used instead. The correlation, its Reynolds-dependent
sub-factors, and the resulting Fanning-form pressure-drop equation are as given in
Eqs.~(Martin\,1)--(Martin\,3); they are not repeated here.

\subsection{Shared Channel Geometry}

The water side is assumed to share the same corrugation family ($\Dh$, $\beta$,
$p_{co}$, and hence $b$) as the refrigerant side of this plate (\S\ref{evap:sec:dh}
--\ref{evap:sec:chspacing}), since both streams flow through the same brazed-plate
pack. The water-side channel count $N_{cp,\mathrm{water}} = 24$ and plate width
$L_w = 200$\,mm match the values used for the economizer's water stream, giving
$A_{\mathrm{flow,water}} = N_{cp,\mathrm{water}}\,b\,L_w \approx 0.0112$\,m$^2$.

\subsection{Implementation}

\texttt{calc\_water\_pressure\_drop()} in \texttt{evaporator.py} calls the same
\texttt{\_martin\_dp()} helper introduced for the economizer, evaluated at the mean
chilled-water temperature (18\,\textdegree C, i.e.\ the average of the 21/15\,\textdegree C
in/out design values) with $\mdot_{\mathrm{water}}$ from the water-side energy balance of
\S2.9. Because the stream is single-phase throughout, there is no zone splitting: the full
plate length $L_v$ is treated as one pass, exactly as in the economizer model.

\section{Thermal Sizing --- LMTD Method}

\subsection{Three-Zone Temperature Profile}

Unlike the evaporator, the condenser refrigerant passes through three distinct zones:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Desuperheating} --- superheated vapour cools from $T_{\mathrm{in}}$
        to $T_{\mathrm{sat}}$ (single-phase).
  \item \textbf{Condensing} --- refrigerant condenses at constant $T_{\mathrm{sat}}$
        (two-phase).
  \item \textbf{Subcooling} --- saturated liquid cools from $T_{\mathrm{sat}}$ to
        $T_{\mathrm{out}}$ (single-phase).
\end{enumerate}

CoolProp-evaluated enthalpies at $p_{\mathrm{in}} = 17.89$\,bar(a):

\subsection{Counter-Flow LMTD Approximation}
\label{cond:sec:lmtd}

\[
  \Tlm = \frac{\Delta T_1 - \Delta T_2}{\ln(\Delta T_1 / \Delta T_2)}
\]

\begin{table}[h!]

\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lll}

  \Tlm = \frac{26 - 7}{\ln(26/7)} = \frac{19}{1.313} = 14.5\;\text{\textdegree C}

\[
  A = \frac{Q}{U\,\Tlm} = \frac{190{,}000}{2{,}500 \times 14.5} = 5.24\;\mathrm{m^2}
\]

$\mathbf{6.3}$\,\textbf{m}$^2$.

\subsection{Subcooling Justification}
\label{cond:sec:subcool}

A design subcooling of 5\,K is adopted because: (1) it prevents flash gas at the EEV
inlet, which disrupts mass-flow calibration; (2) the project brief neglects liquid-line
pressure drop so the full 5\,K is delivered to the valve; (3) subcooling increases the
refrigerating effect and improves COP by $\approx$1--2\,\% for R-290 at this level; and
(4) 5\,K is a common industry default with diminishing returns beyond this value.

\subsection{Reasonableness of $U = 2{,}500\;\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-1}}$}

For a refrigerant-to-glycol BPHE condenser, 30\,\% propylene glycol has higher viscosity
and lower thermal conductivity than pure water, suppressing the secondary-side film
coefficient. Typical $U$ for refrigerant condensing against glycol falls in the range
2{,}000--3{,}500\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ (vs.\ 2{,}500--4{,}500 for water). The value of
2{,}500\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ is therefore reasonable and slightly conservative.

% ── §3.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Design Pressure Determination}

The condenser sits on the \emph{high side} of the refrigerant circuit. At 52\,\textdegree C
condensing, R-290 pressure is 17.89\,bar(a) / 16.87\,bar(g) --- already higher than the
evaporator's operating pressure. The \emph{standstill condition} still governs: at
55\,\textdegree C, R-290 reaches 19.07\,bar(a) / 18.06\,bar(g), above the operating
pressure. The required design pressure ($>$18\,bar(g)) is identical to the evaporator;
45\,bar standard ratings comfortably satisfy it.

% ── §3.4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Heat Exchanger Technology and Safety Considerations}

\subsection{Flammable Refrigerant (A3) and Glycol-Loop Considerations}

The argument for double-wall construction is at least as strong here as for the
evaporator. The glycol loop connects the BPHE to the \emph{outdoor} dry cooler via
exposed pipework. A single-wall leak would allow R-290 to migrate into the glycol loop
and potentially accumulate near the dry-cooler enclosure. Under EN~378, ASHRAE~15, and
IEC~60335-2-89, DW construction is required whenever an A3 refrigerant is separated from
a secondary fluid that may carry a leak to a location where ignition sources or personnel
may be present. DW construction is therefore adopted for the condenser.

\section{Role of the Pressure-Drop Model}

\texttt{calc\_pressure\_drop()} in \texttt{condenser.py} computes the total frictional
pressure drop across the three zones and sets
\texttt{self.p\_out = self.p\_in - self.delta\_p}. This sets the receiver/EEV inlet
pressure for cycle closure. The method also provides \texttt{Q}, \texttt{UA},
\texttt{LMTD}, \texttt{m\_dot\_glycol}, and \texttt{cp\_glycol} for the dry-cooler loop
and overall cycle balance.

  \qquad G_c = \frac{\mdot}{N_{cp}\,b\,L_w}
  \tag{Eq.\,27,\,28}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  Re_{\mathrm{Eq}} = \frac{G_{\mathrm{Eq}}\,\Dh}{\mu_f}
  \tag{Eq.\,26}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Two-Phase Friction Factor Correlation}

\begin{align}
  f    &= Ge_3 \cdot Re_{\mathrm{Eq}}^{\,Ge_4} \tag{Eq.\,23} \\[6pt]
  Ge_3 &= 3521.1 \times \!\left(\frac{p_{co}}{\Dh}\right)^{\!+4.17}
           \!\times\! \left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta\right)^{\!-7.75} \tag{Eq.\,24} \\[6pt]
  Ge_4 &= -1.024 \times \!\left(\frac{p_{co}}{\Dh}\right)^{\!+0.0925}
           \!\times\! \left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\beta\right)^{\!-1.3} \tag{Eq.\,25}
\end{align}

Valid for $Re_{\mathrm{Eq}} \in [300, 4{,}000]$ (explicitly stated by the authors;
the evaporation paper does not state this limit).

\subsection{Frictional Pressure Drop}

\begin{equation}
  \Delta P_{\mathrm{fr}} = f \times \frac{L_v \times N}{\Dh}
                           \times \frac{G_{\mathrm{Eq}}^2}{\rho_f}
  \tag{Eq.\,12}
\end{equation}

where $N = 2$ is the test-rig calibration constant (see Section~\ref{cond:sec:Ncal}).

\subsection{Validity Ranges}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\beta$: 20\textdegree--45\textdegree; $Re_{\mathrm{Eq}}$: 300--4{,}000;
        $G$: 13--34\,kg\,m$^{-2}$s$^{-1}$; $x$: 0.15--0.9; $T_{\mathrm{cond}}$:
        20--30\,\textdegree C.
  \item Fluids: R410A and R22 --- not R290. Transfer to propane is treated as reasonable
        but has not been independently validated.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation in \texttt{condenser.py}}

\subsection{Three-Zone Structure}

\texttt{calc\_pressure\_drop()} divides the path into three zones by enthalpy:

\[
  L_{\mathrm{dsh}} = L \cdot \frac{h_{\mathrm{in}} - h_g}{h_{\mathrm{in}} - h_{\mathrm{out}}}
  \;(14.0\;\%),\quad
  L_{\mathrm{cond}} = L \cdot \frac{h_g - h_f}{h_{\mathrm{in}} - h_{\mathrm{out}}}
  \;(81.5\;\%),\quad
  L_{\mathrm{sub}} = L \cdot \frac{h_f - h_{\mathrm{out}}}{h_{\mathrm{in}} - h_{\mathrm{out}}}
  \;(4.5\;\%)
\]

\subsection{Condensing-Zone Integration}

The local pressure gradient is integrated across 200 quality steps ($x = 0 \to 1$) using
\texttt{np.trapezoid}, capturing the continuous variation of $G_{\mathrm{Eq}}$,
$Re_{\mathrm{Eq}}$, and $f$ throughout the condensing zone.

\subsection{Single-Phase Zones (Desuperheating and Subcooling)}

Both single-phase zones use Blasius friction ($16/Re$ for $Re < 2000$;
$0.079\,Re^{-0.25}$ for turbulent). Fluid properties are evaluated at the average
enthalpy of each zone via CoolProp. Pressure drop:

\[
  \Delta P_{\mathrm{zone}} = 2f\,\frac{G^2}{\rho}\,\frac{L_{\mathrm{zone}}}{\Dh}
\]

\subsection{Critical Note: Calibration Constant $N = 2$}
\label{cond:sec:Ncal}

In the Han et al.\ test rig, the BPHE had exactly 2 refrigerant channels ($N = 2$). The
correlation was regressed against those measurements, so $N = 2$ in Eq.\,12 is a
\emph{calibration constant} --- it cannot be replaced by the design $N_{cp}$. The code
uses \texttt{N\_HAN\_CAL = 2}, verified against Fig.\,5 of the paper (4--7\,kPa at
$G_c = 34$\,kg\,m$^{-2}$s$^{-1}$). Substituting the design $N_{cp} = 47$ would overpredict
$\Delta P$ by a factor of 23.5$\times$.

The physical interpretation: in a single-pass BPHE, port-to-port pressure drop is
independent of the number of parallel channels when $G_c$ is fixed. The design $N_{cp}$
enters only through $G_c = \mdot/(N_{cp}\,b\,L_w)$.

\chapter{Free-Cooling Economizer (Waterside Economizer) Plate Heat Exchanger}
\label{ch:econ}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\zonedivider{Part A \quad---\quad Thermal \& Mechanical Sizing and Model Selection}

% ── §4.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Design Basis and Role}

The waterside economizer is a \textbf{glycol-to-chilled-water plate heat exchanger}
installed \emph{in parallel} with the mechanical chiller. When the outdoor air is cold
enough, the dry cooler chills the intermediate 30\,\% propylene-glycol (PG) loop directly,
and this exchanger transfers that ``coolth'' to the chilled water --- cooling it from
21\,\textdegree C to 15\,\textdegree C \textbf{with the compressor switched off}. This is
the free-cooling (``waterside-economizer'') mode required by ASHRAE~90.1-2022~\S6.5.1, which
mandates that the loop be capable of meeting 100\,\% of the design cooling load when active.

\subsection{The Defining Difference: Single-Phase on Both Sides}
\label{econ:sec:singlephase}

Unlike the evaporator (Chapter~\ref{ch:evap}) and condenser (Chapter~\ref{ch:cond}), the
economizer carries \textbf{no refrigerant and undergoes no phase change}. Both streams are
single-phase liquids --- water on one side, 30\,\% PG on the other. Two consequences follow
and they shape the entire chapter:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Thermal profile:} both streams are sensibly sloped (no isothermal boiling
        or condensing plateau), so the counter-flow LMTD uses the true terminal
        differences on both ends.
  \item \textbf{Pressure drop:} the Han, Lee \& Kim (2003) \emph{two-phase} correlation used
        in the previous two chapters is physically inapplicable. It is replaced by the
        single-phase chevron-plate friction correlation of \textbf{Martin (1996)}
        (Part~B, \S\ref{econ:sec:martin}), evaluated independently on each stream.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Free-Cooling Temperature Cascade}
\label{econ:sec:cascade}

The economizer must be sized at the \emph{most demanding} free-cooling condition: the
highest outdoor air temperature (OAT) at which 100\,\% free cooling is still required,
namely \textbf{OAT $= 4$\,\textdegree C} (per the project's ``full free cooling
$\leq 4$\,\textdegree C'' threshold). At colder OAT the glycol is colder, the approach is
larger, and sizing is easier. The design cascade from ambient air to chilled-water supply
is:

\[
  \underbrace{4\,\text{\textdegree C}}_{\text{OAT}}
  \;\xrightarrow{\;+5\,\text{K dry-cooler approach}\;}\;
  \underbrace{9\,\text{\textdegree C}}_{\text{glycol supply}}
  \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{economizer}\;}\;
  \underbrace{15\,\text{\textdegree C}}_{\text{glycol return}}
  \qquad\Bigg\|\qquad
  \underbrace{21\,\text{\textdegree C}}_{\text{CHW return}}
  \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{economizer}\;}\;
  \underbrace{15\,\text{\textdegree C}}_{\text{CHW supply}}
\]

The 5\,K dry-cooler approach is consistent with the value adopted for chiller-mode heat
rejection (35\,\textdegree C air $\to$ 40\,\textdegree C glycol) in Chapter~\ref{ch:cond}.
A 6\,K glycol rise is chosen to mirror the 6\,K chilled-water span, giving a balanced,
symmetric exchanger.

\section{Thermal Sizing --- LMTD Method}

Both streams are single-phase and counter-flow, so:

\[
  \Tlm = \frac{\Delta T_1 - \Delta T_2}{\ln(\Delta T_1 / \Delta T_2)}
\]

\begin{table}[h!]
\caption{Terminal temperature differences (both ends balanced by design).}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Terminal} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule

\end{tabularx}

With both terminals equal, the LMTD reduces to its limiting value $\Tlm = 6.0$\,K:

\[
  UA = \frac{Q}{\Tlm} = \frac{150{,}000}{6.0} = 25.0\;\mathrm{kW\,K^{-1}},
  \qquad
  A = \frac{Q}{U\,\Tlm} = \frac{150{,}000}{3{,}500 \times 6.0} = 7.14\;\mathrm{m^2}
\]

Adding $\approx$20\,\% margin gives a practical target of approximately
$\mathbf{8.6}$\,\textbf{m}$^2$. This is the \emph{largest} of the three plant heat
exchangers (cf.\ 4.66\,m$^2$ evaporator, 5.24\,m$^2$ condenser) --- the direct consequence
of the small 6\,K approach, which is the characteristic signature of a waterside economizer.

\subsection{Reasonableness of $U = 3{,}500\;\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-1}}$}

For a single-phase liquid-to-liquid BPHE, published overall coefficients fall in the
3{,}000--6{,}000\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ range. The glycol side (30\,\% PG, higher viscosity
and lower conductivity than water) suppresses its film coefficient, so a value at the
lower-middle of the band is appropriate. At the design channel velocities of
$\approx$0.43--0.45\,m\,s$^{-1}$ (Part~B), $U = 3{,}500$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ is a
reasonable, slightly conservative preliminary assumption.

% ── §4.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Design Pressure Determination}

This is the one component in the plant that \textbf{never contacts refrigerant}. Both sides
carry the low-pressure glycol/water loop, which is a pumped circuit operating at only a few
bar. There is no R-290 standstill pressure to contain and no A3 flammable inventory to
separate. The governing design pressure is therefore set by the hydraulic loop and pump
shut-off head (typically $\leq$10\,bar), \emph{not} by the 18--20\,bar(g) refrigerant
standstill that dictated the evaporator and condenser. A standard \textbf{16\,bar}
plate-heat-exchanger rating is fully sufficient.

% ── §4.4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Heat Exchanger Technology and Selection}

\subsection{Double-Wall Construction is NOT Required Here}

The evaporator and condenser adopted 45\,bar \emph{double-wall} (DW) Kelvion plates
specifically because an A3 refrigerant (R-290) is separated from a secondary fluid.
The economizer separates \emph{glycol from water} --- two benign secondary fluids, neither
flammable. None of the EN~378 / ASHRAE~15 / IEC~60335 double-wall triggers apply. The
economizer can therefore use a cheaper, \textbf{single-wall, standard-pressure} plate heat
exchanger. This is a genuine cost and availability advantage and is the correct engineering
call for this duty.

\subsection{Why a Larger Frame Than the 500H}

Two drivers push the economizer to a larger frame than the DW~500H used elsewhere:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Area.} The margined target ($\approx$8.6\,m$^2$) exceeds what a single
        DW~500H can deliver: at $\approx$0.0665\,m$^2$ per plate and a 100-plate limit its
        ceiling is $\approx$6.65\,m$^2$. Meeting 8.6\,m$^2$ would require two 500H units in
        parallel.
  \item \textbf{Flow / ports.} The economizer passes the full chilled-water flow
        (21.5\,m$^3$/h) \emph{plus} a comparable glycol flow (22.8\,m$^3$/h) --- roughly
        double the per-unit throughput of the refrigerant-side exchangers. The 500H-class
        G1\,\textonequarter/G2\,\textonehalf\ ports would run at excessive port velocity;
        a larger frame with G3 ports is needed.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Selected: a single-wall Kelvion GB-series plate HX on a larger
($\approx$0.7\,m tall, G3-port) frame}, using the same chevron corrugation family as the
evaporator/condenser (so plate technology and correlations carry over). A representative
larger plate ($L_w = 0.25$\,m, $L_v = 0.70$\,m, $\approx$0.175\,m$^2$/plate outer footprint)
meets the 8.6\,m$^2$ margined area in \textbf{$\approx$49 plates} --- comfortably within a
single frame. (If strict spare-parts commonality is preferred over cost, two DW~500H units
in parallel are the fallback.)

% ── §4.5 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Full vs.\ Partial Economizer Operation}

The economizer is sized for \emph{full} free cooling at OAT $\leq 4$\,\textdegree C, but
operates in three regimes set by the ASHRAE~90.1 thresholds:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OAT $> 10$\,\textdegree C --- mechanical cooling.} Glycol from the dry
        cooler is too warm to help; the economizer is bypassed and the chiller carries the
        full load.
  \item \textbf{$4 < \text{OAT} \leq 10$\,\textdegree C --- integrated (partial) free
        cooling.} The dry cooler delivers glycol between $\approx$9 and 15\,\textdegree C.
        The economizer pre-cools the returning chilled water and the chiller trims the
        remainder, reducing compressor load and lift.
  \item \textbf{OAT $\leq 4$\,\textdegree C --- full free cooling.} Glycol reaches
        $\leq 9$\,\textdegree C, the economizer carries the entire 150\,kW, and the
        compressor is off. This is the sizing point of this chapter.
\end{itemize}

For the Champaign, IL (TMY3, ASHRAE climate zone 5A) location, a large fraction of the
8{,}760 annual hours sit below the 10\,\textdegree C activation threshold, so the
economizer contributes a substantial share of annual cooling and materially improves the
facility PUE (evaluated in the seasonal-performance section of the main report).

% ── Part B divider ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\zonedivider{Part B \quad---\quad Single-Phase Pressure-Drop Model}

% ── §4.6 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Role of the Pressure-Drop Model}

\texttt{calc\_pressure\_drops()} in \texttt{economizer.py} computes the frictional pressure
drop on \emph{each} stream separately. Unlike the refrigerant-side drops of the evaporator
and condenser (which feed back into the compressor suction/discharge state and hence the
COP), the economizer drops set the \textbf{pump heads} of the chilled-water and glycol
loops. Keeping them low is central to free cooling: any pump energy spent circulating the
loops erodes the very saving that switching the compressor off is meant to deliver.

% ── §4.7 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Source Correlation: Martin (1996) --- Single-Phase Chevron Friction}
\label{econ:sec:martin}

\subsection{Why Not Han, Lee \& Kim (2003)?}

The Han correlations are \emph{two-phase} friction factors, regressed against evaporating /
condensing refrigerant and formulated in terms of an equivalent mass flux $G_{\mathrm{Eq}}$
that blends liquid and vapour densities across vapour quality. With no vapour present in
either economizer stream, $G_{\mathrm{Eq}}$ collapses and the correlation is out of its
domain. A single-phase correlation is required.

\subsection{The Martin (1996) Generalised L\'ev\^eque Correlation}

Martin's correlation (H.\ Martin, ``A theoretical approach to predict the performance of
chevron-type plate heat exchangers,'' \emph{Chem.\ Eng.\ Process.} 35 (1996) 301--310; also
VDI Heat Atlas, Sec.~N6) is the standard single-phase friction model for chevron plates. The
Fanning-type friction factor $f$ is:

\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} =
  \frac{\cos\varphi}{\sqrt{0.045\tan\varphi + 0.09\sin\varphi + f_0/\cos\varphi}}
  + \frac{1-\cos\varphi}{\sqrt{3.8\,f_1}}
  \tag{Martin\,1}
\end{equation}

with the smooth- and corrugated-channel sub-factors evaluated per Reynolds range:

\begin{align}
  Re < 2000:\quad   & f_0 = \frac{16}{Re}, & f_1 &= \frac{149}{Re} + 0.9625 \tag{Martin\,2a}\\[4pt]
  Re \geq 2000:\quad& f_0 = \bigl(1.56\ln Re - 3.0\bigr)^{-2}, & f_1 &= \frac{9.75}{Re^{0.289}} \tag{Martin\,2b}
\end{align}

The frictional pressure drop then uses the same Fanning form already adopted for the
single-phase zones of the evaporator/condenser:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta P = 2\,f\,\frac{L_v}{\Dh}\,\frac{G^2}{\rho},
  \qquad G = \frac{\mdot}{N_{cp}\,b\,L_w}
  \tag{Martin\,3}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Chevron-Angle Convention}

$\varphi$ is the chevron angle measured from the \emph{longitudinal (flow) axis}, so that a
larger angle yields a harder plate and more friction. This is the same sense in which
$\beta$ enters Han's two-phase correlation (friction increases with $\beta$), so the
project value $\beta = 30$\textdegree\ is used \textbf{directly} as $\varphi$, with no
$90\text{\textdegree}-\beta$ conversion. The code flags this on one line so it can be flipped
to $(90\text{\textdegree}-\beta)$ if a vendor drawing defines the angle from the horizontal.

\subsection{Validity}

Equation~(Martin\,1) is valid across laminar and turbulent regimes ($Re \sim 1$ to
$>10^4$) and chevron angles up to 80\textdegree, and --- being single-phase --- is
fluid-agnostic (no R-290-transfer caveat, unlike Han). At the design point the streams sit
at $Re \approx 1{,}600$ (water) and $\approx 460$ (glycol), well inside the correlation's
range.

% ── §4.8 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Implementation in \texttt{economizer.py}}

\subsection{Per-Stream Evaluation}

The private helper \texttt{\_martin\_dp(m\_dot, A\_flow, fluid, T\_avg)} evaluates
Eqs.~(Martin\,1)--(Martin\,3) for one stream: it pulls $\rho$ and $\mu$ from CoolProp at the
stream's mean temperature, forms the per-channel mass flux $G = \mdot/A_{\mathrm{flow}}$
with $A_{\mathrm{flow}} = N_{cp}\,b\,L_w$, computes $Re$, selects the laminar or turbulent
branch, and returns $\Delta P$. \texttt{calc\_pressure\_drops()} calls it twice --- once for
water, once for glycol --- so each side gets its own $G$, $Re$, $f$ and $\Delta P$.

\subsection{No Zone Splitting}

Because neither stream changes phase, there is no boiling/condensing/desuperheating zone
decomposition: each stream is a single sensible pass over the full plate length $L_v$. This
makes the economizer model markedly simpler than the three-zone condenser model.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

% ── §5.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Design Basis and Role}

The dry cooler is the final heat-rejection step of the glycol loop: an outdoor,
finned-tube, air-cooled heat exchanger with variable-speed axial fans that rejects heat
from the 30\,\% propylene-glycol (PG) loop directly to ambient air. It operates at two
qualitatively different duties depending on the mode of the plant:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mechanical mode.} The compressor is running; the dry cooler rejects both
        the evaporator's refrigerating effect \emph{and} the compressor's shaft work,
        received from the condenser (Chapter~\ref{ch:cond}) as hot glycol at
        46\,\textdegree C.
  \item \textbf{Free-cooling mode.} The compressor is off; the dry cooler instead supplies
        cold glycol directly to the economizer (Chapter~\ref{ch:econ}), rejecting only the
        bare 150\,kW IT/cooling load with no compressor-work addition.
\end{itemize}

These are sized and checked as two \emph{separate} operating points on the \emph{same}
physical coil --- a single piece of hardware, evaluated twice.

\subsection{Why a Different Method Than the Plate Heat Exchangers}
\label{dry:sec:whydifferent}

The evaporator, condenser, and economizer (Chapters~\ref{ch:evap}--\ref{ch:econ}) are all
single-pass, counter-flow, chevron-plate heat exchangers, sized by LMTD and evaluated with
the Han, Lee \& Kim (2003) two-phase or Martin (1996) single-phase chevron-plate friction
correlations. None of that carries over to the dry cooler, because it is a fundamentally
different exchanger type:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flow arrangement.} Air crosses a finned round-tube bundle
        \emph{perpendicular} to the glycol flow, over several tube rows --- a
        \textbf{cross-flow} arrangement, not counter-current. A single LMTD systematically
        mis-predicts duty for this geometry.
  \item \textbf{Geometry.} Both plate-friction correlations are regressed against chevron
        \emph{plates}; neither applies to air flowing over finned \emph{round tubes}.
  \item \textbf{A fan, not a pump, drives the air side}, and it is variable-speed --- a new
        degree of freedom (and a new energy term for the facility PUE) that the plate
        exchangers do not have.
\end{itemize}

This chapter therefore uses the \textbf{effectiveness--NTU method} (cross-flow, both fluids
unmixed) for the thermal duty, \textbf{fan affinity laws} for the variable-speed fan power,
and (Part~B) a \textbf{vendor-anchored scaling method} for the glycol-side pressure drop
rather than an independent friction correlation.

% ── §5.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Governing Method: Cross-Flow Effectiveness--NTU}
\label{dry:sec:epsntu}

For a cross-flow exchanger with both fluids unmixed, the standard approximation
(Incropera, \emph{Fundamentals of Heat and Mass Transfer}) is:

\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = 1 - \exp\left\{\frac{1}{C_r}\,NTU^{0.22}
  \left[\exp\!\left(-C_r\,NTU^{0.78}\right) - 1\right]\right\},
  \qquad C_r = \frac{C_{\min}}{C_{\max}}
  \tag{$\varepsilon$-NTU}
  \label{eq:epsntu}
\end{equation}

with $C = \mdot\,c_p$ the capacity rate of each stream. \textbf{Sizing} (mechanical-mode
design point, \S\ref{dry:sec:mechdesign}) uses Eq.~\eqref{eq:epsntu} \emph{backward}: the
required $\varepsilon$ is computed from the duty and terminal temperatures, and $NTU$ ---
which cannot be isolated algebraically from Eq.~\eqref{eq:epsntu} --- is obtained by
numerical root-finding (\texttt{scipy.optimize.brentq}), after which $UA = NTU\cdot C_{\min}$.
\textbf{Off-design checking} (free-cooling point, \S\ref{dry:sec:freecool}) uses the same
equation \emph{forward}: holding $UA$ fixed (scaled for reduced air flow), a second
root-finding search finds the air mass flow that reproduces a target duty at new terminal
temperatures. Both uses are implemented in \texttt{dry\_cooler.py}
(\texttt{size\_design\_point()} and \texttt{predict\_off\_design()} respectively).

% ── §5.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Mechanical-Mode Design Point}
\label{dry:sec:mechdesign}

\subsection{Required Duty}

The dry cooler must reject both the IT/cooling load and the compressor's shaft work:

\[
  Q_{\mathrm{cond}} = Q_{\mathrm{evap}} + W_{\mathrm{comp}}
  = 150\,\mathrm{kW} + W_{\mathrm{comp}}
\]

Targeting the project's minimum COP requirement of 3.5 gives an upper bound on compressor
work, $W_{\mathrm{comp}} \leq 150/3.5 \approx 42.9$\,kW, and hence
$Q_{\mathrm{cond}} \lesssim 193$\,kW --- rounded to a \textbf{190\,kW} preliminary design
duty (Chapter~\ref{ch:cond}). \textbf{This number is provisional}: it is derived backward
from the COP \emph{requirement}, not from a completed compressor model, and must be
reconfirmed once the compressor chapter (pending) supplies a real $W_{\mathrm{comp}}$ at the
actual operating point.

free-cooling check at the economizer's 6.52\,kg/s flow instead of the fixed design value.
This is flagged here rather than silently resolved, since the correct answer depends on a
glycol-pump control-strategy decision that has not yet been made elsewhere in the report.

% ── §5.8 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Fan Operation, Variable-Speed Control, and Annual Energy}
\label{dry:sec:fans}

\subsection{Affinity-Law Fan Power}

The selected unit's 4 EC (electronically commutated) fans are natively variable-speed,
matching the fan-speed-modulation control strategy assumed throughout this chapter. Fan
power follows the standard affinity laws:

\[
  \dot V \propto N, \qquad \Delta p \propto N^2, \qquad P_{\mathrm{fan}} \propto N^3
\]

so that turning the fans down to the 54.5\,\% speed found in \S\ref{dry:sec:freecool}
draws only $\approx 0.545^3 \approx 16\,\%$ of rated power --- the physical basis for why
variable-speed fan control materially improves annual facility PUE relative to fixed-speed
operation.

% ── §5.10 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Role of the Pressure-Drop Model}

As with the economizer (Chapter~\ref{ch:econ}, \S\ref{econ:sec:martin} onward), the dry
cooler's glycol-side pressure drop does not feed back into the refrigeration cycle's COP
(the dry cooler carries only glycol, never refrigerant); instead it sets the
\textbf{glycol pump head} required to circulate the loop, which is itself an energy
consumer in the facility's total (and hence part of PUE, alongside compressor and fan
energy).

\subsection{Why Not an Independent Friction Correlation}

Unlike the plate exchangers, where $D_h$, $\beta$, $b$, $L_w$, $L_v$ are catalog
parameters the model can use directly with the Han or Martin correlations, the dry
cooler's internal tube-circuit \emph{routing} (exact run length per circuit, number of
bends) is proprietary manufacturing detail that Kelvion does not publish. An early attempt
at an independent Darcy--Weisbach estimate (real circuit count, tube diameter back-solved
from the vendor's reported internal velocity, but a \emph{guessed} tube run length) came
out 75\,\% low against the vendor's real value (11.94\,kPa modelled vs.\ 48.26\,kPa
reported) --- a clear illustration that the missing tube-length input, not the underlying
physics, was the source of error. This was replaced with the vendor-anchored scaling
method below, which needs no tube-geometry input at all.

% ── §5.11 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Vendor-Anchored Pressure-Drop Scaling}
\label{dry:sec:dpscaling}

\subsection{Derivation}

For turbulent flow in a smooth circular tube, Darcy--Weisbach with the Blasius friction
factor gives:

\[
  \Delta P = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{1}{2}\rho\,v^2,
  \qquad f = 0.184\,Re^{-0.2},
  \qquad Re = \frac{\rho\,v\,D}{\mu}
\]

Substituting $f$ and collecting terms:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta P \;\propto\; \rho^{0.8}\,v^{1.8}\,\mu^{0.2}
  \qquad \text{(for fixed tube geometry } L, D\text{)}
  \tag{Scaling\,1}
  \label{eq:scaling1}
\end{equation}

For a fixed flow area (i.e.\ the \emph{same physical coil}, same $N_{\mathrm{circuits}}$
and tube diameter), velocity scales as $v \propto \mdot/\rho$, so the ratio of pressure
drops between two operating conditions \emph{on the same coil} is:

\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Delta P_2}{\Delta P_1} =
  \left(\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\right)^{0.8}
  \left(\frac{\mdot_2\,\rho_1}{\mdot_1\,\rho_2}\right)^{1.8}
  \left(\frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1}\right)^{0.2}
  \tag{Scaling\,2}
  \label{eq:scaling2}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Crucially, the unknown tube length $L$, diameter $D$, and circuit count
$N_{\mathrm{circuits}}$ all cancel exactly} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling2}. Given one real,
vendor-reported pressure drop at a reference condition, Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling2} scales it
to \emph{any other} condition on the same coil using only $\mdot$, $\rho$, and $\mu$ ---
all directly available from CoolProp, with no geometric assumption required. This is
implemented as \texttt{DryCooler.scale\_pressure\_drop()}.

\subsection{Application: Design Point $\to$ Free-Cooling Point}

\begin{table}[h!]
\caption{Vendor-anchored pressure-drop scaling: mechanical design point $\to$ free-cooling point.}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lLL}
\toprule
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Design point (ref.)} & \textbf{Free-cooling point} \\
\midrule

\midrule

\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent The scaling ratio $\Delta P_2/\Delta P_1 = 0.668$. Two competing effects
determine this result: the \emph{lower flow rate} at the free-cooling point pushes
$\Delta P$ down, while the \emph{nearly 3$\times$ higher glycol viscosity} at the colder
average temperature pushes it up. The flow-rate effect dominates, so the free-cooling
pressure drop is \emph{lower} than the mechanical-mode design point despite the glycol
being noticeably more viscous --- a result worth stating explicitly, since ``colder
$\Rightarrow$ lower pressure drop'' is not obvious on inspection.

\subsection{Validity Caveat}

The Reynolds number at the design point ($Re \approx 9{,}500$) is comfortably turbulent,
but at the free-cooling point it drops to $Re \approx 2{,}400$ --- close to the
laminar/turbulent transition ($Re \approx 2{,}300$--$4{,}000$), where the Blasius
correlation underlying Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling1} is not rigorously validated. The scaled
32.22\,kPa should therefore be treated as a \textbf{reasonable extrapolation}, not an
exact result, pending vendor confirmation at that specific operating point (see
\S\ref{dry:sec:openitems}).

\emph{Note:} the glycol mass flow used for the free-cooling scaling above (6.52\,kg/s,
consistent with the economizer's own design basis) differs from the fixed-pump flow
(9.07\,kg/s) used in the thermal free-cooling check of \S\ref{dry:sec:freecool} --- the
same glycol-flow inconsistency flagged in \S\ref{dry:sec:openflow} propagates here and
should be resolved together with that item.

\section{Air-Side Pressure Drop --- Scope Note}

Air-side pressure drop across the finned tube bundle (and hence the fan's required

(\S\ref{dry:sec:datasheet}) reports \textbf{0.00\,inWg external static pressure} at the

static pressure (accounting for ducting, louvers, or hail guards, if any) would need to be
supplied back into the Select RT tool for a final fan-curve check.

\chapter{Electronic Expansion Valve (EEV)}
\label{ch:eev}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\zonedivider{Part A \quad---\quad Isenthalpic Throttling Model}

% ── §6.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Design Basis and Role}

The electronic expansion valve (EEV) is the fourth and final component of the refrigerant
loop, closing the cycle between the condenser (Chapter~\ref{ch:cond}) and the evaporator
(Chapter~\ref{ch:evap}): subcooled liquid R-290 leaving the condenser at the high
(condensing) pressure is throttled down to the low (evaporating) pressure, flashing
partially to vapour in the process, before entering the evaporator. In operation the EEV
modulates its orifice opening to hold a target evaporator superheat; \textbf{this chapter
models the steady design point only} --- the superheat control loop is a separate
discussion item, noted in \S\ref{eev:sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Why Isenthalpic, Not a Pressure-Drop Correlation}
\label{eev:sec:isenthalpic}

Unlike every other component in this report, the EEV needs no Han (2003) or Martin (1996)
friction correlation at all. Throttling across a valve is \textbf{adiabatic and does no
shaft work}, so the steady-flow energy balance collapses to:

\[
  h_{\mathrm{out}} = h_{\mathrm{in}}
\]

The pressure drop across the valve is not a frictional loss to be \emph{modelled} ---
it is the entire \emph{design intent} of the component: the valve exists specifically to
create the pressure difference between the condensing and evaporating sides of the cycle.
The valve's mechanical sizing (Part~B) determines \emph{how large an orifice} produces a
given mass flow at a given $\Delta P$, not the $\Delta P$ itself, which is fixed
externally by the condensing and evaporating pressures the rest of the cycle establishes.

% ── §6.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Inlet State: Subcooled Liquid from the Condenser}
\label{eev:sec:inlet}

The EEV inlet state is taken directly as the condenser outlet state
(Chapter~\ref{ch:cond}), with no liquid-line pressure drop modelled between the two
components (a standard simplifying assumption for a short, well-insulated liquid line;
flagged in \S\ref{eev:sec:limitations}).

\texttt{calc\_inlet\_state()} recovers the valve-inlet temperature and subcooling from

\[
  T_{\mathrm{in}} = T(h_{\mathrm{in}}, p_{\mathrm{in}}),
  \qquad
  \Delta T_{\mathrm{sub}} = T_{\mathrm{bubble}}(p_{\mathrm{in}}) - T_{\mathrm{in}}
\]

\subsection{Consistency Check Against the Condenser Chapter}
\label{eev:sec:consistency}

Evaluating this independently returns $T_{\mathrm{in}} = 47.01$\,\textdegree C and
$\Delta T_{\mathrm{sub}} = 4.99$\,K --- matching the condenser chapter's stated 47\,\textdegree C
outlet temperature and 5\,K design subcooling (\S\ref{cond:sec:subcool}) to within
rounding. This is a genuine cross-chapter validation: the condenser and EEV models were
built independently but agree on the same thermodynamic state, confirming the enthalpy
value carried between the two chapters is internally consistent.

\label{eev:sec:outlet}

(Chapter~\ref{ch:evap}), and the outlet enthalpy is, by the energy balance of
\S\ref{eev:sec:isenthalpic}, unchanged from the inlet:

\subsection{Interpreting the 30\,\% Flash Gas Fraction}

Roughly 30\,\% of the refrigerant mass flashes to vapour \emph{immediately upon throttling},
before any heat is absorbed in the evaporator. This is normal and expected: the liquid
entering the valve at 47\,\textdegree C is far hotter than the 7\,\textdegree C saturation
temperature on the low-pressure side, so a large fraction of the enthalpy difference is
released as flash gas rather than being available for useful refrigeration. This directly
motivates the condenser's 5\,K design subcooling (Chapter~\ref{ch:cond},
\S\ref{cond:sec:subcool}): every additional kelvin of subcooling reduces the flash-gas
fraction and increases the refrigerating effect delivered per unit mass flow, at the cost
of a larger condenser subcooling zone.

% ── Part B divider ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\zonedivider{Part B \quad---\quad Orifice / Mechanical Sizing}

% ── §6.4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Role of the Orifice Sizing Model}

Sections~\ref{eev:sec:inlet}--\ref{eev:sec:outlet} establish the thermodynamic states either
side of the valve; they say nothing about the \emph{physical valve} needed to produce that
pressure drop at the required mass flow. Part~B closes that gap with a first-cut orifice
flow area, which is the number actually used to shortlist a real EEV from a manufacturer's
capacity/$K_v$ tables (e.g.\ Danfoss ETS, Emerson/Alco EX series).

% ── §6.5 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Incompressible Orifice Flow Model}
\label{eev:sec:orifice}

\texttt{calc\_orifice()} uses the standard incompressible-liquid orifice equation,
evaluated at the liquid inlet state:

\begin{equation}
  \mdot_r = C_d\,A_{\mathrm{orifice}}\,\sqrt{2\,\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\,\Delta P}
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  A_{\mathrm{orifice}} = \frac{\mdot_r}{C_d\sqrt{2\,\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\,\Delta P}}
  \tag{Orifice\,1}
  \label{eq:orifice}
\end{equation}

with $\rho_{\mathrm{in}}$ the subcooled liquid density at the valve inlet (from CoolProp,
$\rho_{\mathrm{in}} = 455.6$\,kg/m$^3$ at the design state).

\subsection{Why This Is a First-Cut Estimate, Not a Final Selection}
\label{eev:sec:whyfirstcut}

Equation~\eqref{eq:orifice} assumes single-phase incompressible flow through the orifice.
In reality, the flow begins flashing \emph{inside} the valve port itself (the 30.1\,\%
outlet quality of \S\ref{eev:sec:outlet} does not appear instantaneously at the valve exit
plane; some of it forms within the throttling passage), which increases the specific
volume and alters the effective discharge behaviour relative to a purely liquid orifice.
Manufacturers account for this with two-phase-corrected $K_v$/capacity ratings, obtained
from flow-bench testing rather than derived analytically. Equation~\eqref{eq:orifice}
therefore gives a \textbf{flow-area estimate suitable for an initial vendor shortlist},
not a substitute for that vendor's own capacity table.

% ── §6.7 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Limitations and Open Items}
\label{eev:sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Superheat control is not modelled here.} The EEV's real function is to
        \emph{modulate} its orifice opening to hold a target evaporator superheat under
        varying load --- a dynamic control problem, distinct from the single steady design
        point analysed in this chapter. A control-loop discussion (target superheat,
        response time, hunting/stability) is a separate item for the compressor/controls
        chapter once available.
  \item \textbf{No liquid-line pressure drop.} The valve inlet state is taken directly as
        the condenser outlet state, with no allowance for pipe friction or elevation change
        in the liquid line between the two components. The liquid line's diameter is now
        sized in Chapter~\ref{ch:piping} (\S\ref{piping:sec:liquid}), but its length and
        fittings remain unspecified per the project brief's scope; reasonable for a short,
        insulated run, but should be revisited once an actual piping layout is available.
  \item \textbf{Mass flow is compressor-dependent.} As in Chapters~\ref{ch:evap} and
        \ref{ch:cond}, $\mdot_r = 0.553$\,kg/s is derived from the provisional 190\,kW
        condenser duty, itself backed out from the COP $\geq 3.5$ requirement rather than a
        completed compressor model. Reconfirm once the compressor chapter is available.
  \item \textbf{Orifice sizing is a first-cut liquid-orifice estimate}
        (\S\ref{eev:sec:whyfirstcut}), not a vendor-validated two-phase capacity rating.
        Use $A_{\mathrm{orifice}} = 25.68$\,mm$^2$ / $d_{\mathrm{orifice}} = 5.72$\,mm as a
        starting point for a manufacturer catalog search, not a final selection.
\end{enumerate}

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\chapter{Glycol Loop Circulation Pump}
\label{ch:pump}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\zonedivider{Part A \quad---\quad System Curve, Piping, and Duty Point Derivation}

% ── §7.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Design Basis and Role}

A single, variable-speed (VFD) circulation pump drives the 30\,\% propylene-glycol
intermediate loop. Per the bypass-valve arrangement shown in the system schematic
(Fig.~\ref{fig:setup}), the SAME physical pump serves two distinct circuits depending on
plant operating mode:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mechanical mode}: Dry Cooler $\leftrightarrow$ Condenser
        (Chapters~\ref{ch:dry}, \ref{ch:cond}), compressor running, glycol flow
        $\mdot_{\mathrm{glycol}} = 9.07$\,kg/s.
  \item \textbf{Free-cooling mode}: Dry Cooler $\leftrightarrow$ Economizer
        (Chapters~\ref{ch:dry}, \ref{ch:econ}), compressor off, glycol flow
        $\mdot_{\mathrm{glycol}} = 6.52$\,kg/s.
\end{itemize}

A \textbf{variable-speed} pump (rather than fixed-speed) is adopted specifically to resolve
a cross-chapter inconsistency identified during integration: at a single fixed flow rate,
the dry cooler's free-cooling-mode glycol span fell short of the full
15\,\textdegree C\,$\to$\,9\,\textdegree C economizer design span. Running the pump at
reduced speed in free-cooling mode, matched to the economizer's own 6.52\,kg/s design
flow, restores the intended glycol temperature span in that mode and additionally saves
pump energy (roughly with the cube of speed, per the affinity laws) during the
$\approx$55\,\% of annual hours the plant spends in free-cooling.

% ── §7.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{System Curve}
\label{pump:sec:syscurve}

The pump must overcome the sum of all glycol-side resistances in whichever branch is
active. Two independent system curves therefore exist:

\[
  \Delta P_{\mathrm{sys,mech}}(\mdot) = \Delta P_{\mathrm{dry cooler}}
  + \Delta P_{\mathrm{condenser,glycol}} + \Delta P_{\mathrm{piping}}
\]
\[
  \Delta P_{\mathrm{sys,free}}(\mdot) = \Delta P_{\mathrm{dry cooler,free}}
  + \Delta P_{\mathrm{economizer,glycol}} + \Delta P_{\mathrm{piping}}
\]

Each component term is evaluated using the SAME correlation and geometry already
validated in that component's own chapter --- the condenser term is the Martin (1996)
correlation of \S\ref{cond:sec:martin} (corrected 40/46\,\textdegree C, 9.07\,kg/s design
point), the economizer term is \S\ref{econ:sec:martin}'s value as-is, and the dry cooler

% ── §7.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Piping Assumptions}
\label{pump:sec:piping}

The project brief explicitly scopes piping to \emph{diameters only --- no lengths,
fittings, or supports}. A piping term is nonetheless required to close the pump's system
curve, so the following are adopted as explicit, flagged assumptions (not measured or
specified geometry):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pipe size}: sized for $\approx$1.8\,m/s at the mechanical design flow
        (standard closed-loop hydronic velocity range, 1--2.4\,m/s) $\Rightarrow$
        \textbf{3\,in Schedule 40 steel} (77.9\,mm ID), giving an actual velocity of
        1.88\,m/s.
  \item \textbf{Equivalent length}: \textbf{65\,m} total (supply + return + fittings
        allowance), estimated from the project's actual 8\,m\,$\times$\,12\,m data-hall
        footprint (mechanical-room-to-roof-and-back run for the outdoor dry cooler,
        plus a fittings allowance) --- not a measured value.
  \item \textbf{Friction model}: Darcy--Weisbach with the Swamee--Jain explicit
        approximation to the Colebrook equation,
        \[
          f = \frac{0.25}{\left[\log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7}
              + \dfrac{5.74}{Re^{0.9}}\right)\right]^2}, \qquad
          \Delta P = f\,\frac{L_{\mathrm{eq}}}{D}\,\frac{\rho v^2}{2}
        \]
        with $\varepsilon = 0.045$\,mm (steel).
\end{itemize}

At the mechanical design point, $Re \approx 1.0\times10^5$ (fully turbulent); at the
free-cooling design point, $Re \approx 2.6\times10^4$. Piping accounts for roughly a third
of the total mechanical-mode system head --- the single largest assumption-driven term in
this chapter, and the first item to revisit if real routing geometry becomes available.

% ── §7.4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Selection Margin}

The bare system-curve duty point is margined before vendor selection, to absorb the
piping/geometry assumption uncertainty above without grossly oversizing the pump (a pump
selected far above its true duty runs on the inefficient, cavitation-prone far-left of its
curve). A 10\,\% flow / 18\,\% head margin is applied --- the same philosophy as the 20\,\%
area margin already used for the condenser (\S\ref{cond:sec:lmtd}):

\[
  Q_{\mathrm{select}} = 1.10 \times 32.27 = 35.50\;\mathrm{m^3/h}, \qquad
  H_{\mathrm{select}} = 1.18 \times 9.74 = 11.49\;\mathrm{m}
\]

No separate margin is applied to the free-cooling point; the VFD's speed turndown already
provides ample headroom there.

\section{Open Items and Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Piping equivalent length (65\,m) is an assumption}, not measured or
        specified geometry, sized only from the project's stated 8\,m\,$\times$\,12\,m
        footprint. It is the single largest lever in the system curve (roughly a third of
        total mechanical-mode head) and the first item to revisit if real routing data
        becomes available.
  \item \textbf{Pump efficiency (73\,\%) is read from curve-sheet contour lines, not a
        digitized efficiency map}, and carries more uncertainty than the head-curve data
        it sits alongside; motor efficiency (90\,\%) remains a generic assumption pending
        nameplate data. Both should be confirmed via the manufacturer's ESP-Plus selection
        software or a vendor quotation.
  \item \textbf{Margin at maximum trim.} The selected 7.0\,in trim is the largest available
        for the 2AD frame and operates near the top of that frame's envelope
        (\S\ref{pump:sec:vendor}). Any upward revision to system head --- e.g.\ from a
        finalized piping layout longer than the assumed 65\,m, additional fittings, or a
        different glycol concentration --- may push the duty point outside 2AD's envelope,
        requiring the next frame size (2.5AC).
  \item \textbf{Condenser/economizer channel-count split ($N_{cp,\mathrm{glycol}}=24$)
        remains an assumed placeholder} (\S\ref{cond:sec:martin}, \S\ref{econ:sec:martin}),
        pending vendor-confirmed plate geometry.
  \item \textbf{NPSH has not been checked} against the selected pump's NPSHr, since the
        digitized vendor data did not include an NPSH curve; this should be confirmed once
        the full vendor datasheet is available, particularly given the pump's rooftop
        supply-side elevation relative to the mechanical room.
  \item \textbf{Materials of construction} (mechanical seal elastomer --- EPDM recommended
        for inhibited propylene glycol service --- and wetted metallurgy) have not yet been
        confirmed against the specific glycol product to be used; galvanized wetted
        components should be avoided in glycol service.
\end{enumerate}

\zonedivider{Part A \quad---\quad System Curve, CRAH/Piping Assumptions, and Duty Point}

% ── §8.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Design Basis and Role}

A second, dedicated circulation pump drives the chilled-water (CHW) loop between the
data-hall CRAH/in-row units and the mechanical room. This pump is entirely separate from
the glycol loop pump of Chapter~\ref{ch:pump} --- it never sees glycol, and its duty point
is set by the CHW-side flow and resistances of the evaporator (Chapter~\ref{ch:evap}) and
economizer (Chapter~\ref{ch:econ}) rather than the dry cooler.

As with the glycol loop, the CHW loop has two parallel branches selected by operating
mode:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mechanical (chiller) mode}: CRAH $\leftrightarrow$ Evaporator
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:evap}).
  \item \textbf{Free-cooling mode}: CRAH $\leftrightarrow$ Economizer
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:econ}).
\end{itemize}

Unlike the glycol loop, both branches move the \emph{same} CHW mass flow
($\mdot_{\mathrm{water}} = 5.97$\,kg/s, 21\,\textdegree C\,$\to$\,15\,\textdegree C) since
both HXs are sized for the full 150\,kW design load rather than a reduced free-cooling
flow --- so a single design duty point (rather than two, as in Chapter~\ref{ch:pump})
governs this pump's selection. A variable-speed (VFD) pump is nonetheless adopted, for the
same energy-saving rationale as the glycol pump and to allow future turndown at reduced IT
load (30\,kW minimum, per the project brief).

% ── §8.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{System Curve}
\label{chwpump:sec:syscurve}

The pump must overcome the sum of all CHW-side resistances in whichever branch is active:

\[
  \Delta P_{\mathrm{sys}}(\mdot) = \Delta P_{\mathrm{evap/econ,\,water}}
  + \Delta P_{\mathrm{CRAH}} + \Delta P_{\mathrm{piping}}
\]

The evaporator and economizer water-side terms are the Martin (1996) values already
computed in their own chapters (\S\ref{evap:sec:martin}, \S\ref{econ:sec:martin}); since
both branches carry the same flow, the pump is sized on the higher-resistance branch
(evaporator), with a balancing valve on the economizer branch to match it.

\begin{table}[h!]
\caption{System curve component breakdown at the design flow (5.97\,kg/s, 21.5\,m$^3$/h).}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lL}
\toprule
\textbf{Component} & \textbf{$\Delta P$} \\
\midrule
Evaporator, water side (Martin, worst-case branch) & 7.73\,kPa \\
Economizer, water side (Martin, for reference)      & 6.67\,kPa \\
CRAH coil (\S\ref{chwpump:sec:crah}, \textbf{assumed}) & 50.0\,kPa \\
Piping/fittings (\S\ref{chwpump:sec:crah}, \textbf{assumed}) & 25.0\,kPa \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total (design basis)} & \textbf{82.7\,kPa = 8.45\,m = 27.7\,ft} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

% ── §8.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{CRAH and Piping Assumptions}
\label{chwpump:sec:crah}

Two of the four system-curve terms above are \textbf{not derived from project-specified
or vendor data} and are flagged explicitly:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CRAH coil $\Delta P$ (50.0\,kPa, assumed)}: no CRAH/in-row unit is
        specified anywhere in the project brief, and no vendor selection has been carried
        out for one (unlike the dry cooler, evaporator, condenser, and economizer, which
        all rest on a real Kelvion selection). 50\,kPa is a mid-range placeholder for a
        chilled-water fan-coil/CRAH unit at this flow (typical range 40--70\,kPa); it
        should be replaced with a real vendor coil $\Delta P$ once a CRAH unit is selected
        for the 8\,m\,$\times$\,12\,m, $\approx$25-rack data hall.
  \item \textbf{Piping/fittings (25.0\,kPa, assumed)}: the project brief states that
        \emph{pressure losses within the piping are to be neglected}, in contrast to the
        glycol loop chapter's own 65\,m equivalent-length calculation
        (\S\ref{pump:sec:piping}), which is a deliberate, documented deviation from that
        instruction for the glycol side. Per project decision, this term is
        \textbf{retained here too, as an explicit over-design margin} rather than set to
        zero --- i.e., this chapter intentionally selects against the more conservative
        82.7\,kPa total rather than the $\approx$58\,kPa total that would result from
        following the brief literally and dropping this term. If a real piping layout
        becomes available, this term should be replaced with an actual Darcy--Weisbach
        calculation, following the same method as \S\ref{pump:sec:piping}.
\end{itemize}

These two terms together make up $\approx$91\,\% of the total system head --- by far the
dominant uncertainty in this chapter, and the first items to revisit once real CRAH and
piping data exist.

% ── §8.5b ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Frame Size: Why 1.5AD Rather Than the Glycol Pump's 2AD}
\label{chwpump:sec:frame}

The glycol pump (Chapter~\ref{ch:pump}) uses the \textbf{2AD} frame at its much larger
duty ($\approx$156\,GPM @ $\approx$38\,ft). Re-using the same 2AD casing was considered for
parts commonality, but was rejected: at this pump's much smaller duty
($\approx$95\,GPM @ $\approx$28\,ft), the 2AD's own composite coverage floor is 116\,GPM
(Table~\ref{tab:composite}, Chapter~\ref{ch:pump}) --- meaning our duty point falls
\emph{below} that frame's intended operating window entirely. Digitizing the 2AD curve at
this flow shows the pump would run at only $\approx$40--50\,\% efficiency there (per the
B-261G contour lines), far to the left of its best-efficiency point --- an oversized-pump
penalty that works directly against the project's PUE target every one of the 8760
operating hours.

The smaller \textbf{1.5AD} frame's own composite envelope (66--151\,GPM, 16--50\,ft,
Chapter~\ref{ch:pump}'s Table~\ref{tab:composite}) brackets this pump's duty point
centrally rather than at an extreme, keeping the selected trim much closer to its own
best-efficiency region. The two pumps therefore use the same manufacturer and speed family
(Bell \& Gossett e-1510, 1750\,RPM) but different frame sizes --- a reasonable compromise
between parts commonality and avoiding a chronically oversized, inefficient pump.

flow is throttled proportionally with load (20\,\% of design flow, $\approx$19\,GPM), the
quadratic system curve collapses to $\approx$1.1\,ft of head --- an operating point far
below any of the digitized trim curves' normal range, and likely below the pump's minimum
continuous stable flow (risk of internal recirculation/thrust bearing wear at very low
flow, independent of the VFD's electrical turndown capability). This mirrors the
flow-control-strategy question already open on the glycol side
(\S\ref{pump:sec:vfd}) and should be resolved together with it --- e.g. via a minimum-flow
bypass, a fixed CHW flow with only the load-side $\Delta T$ varying, or a second, smaller
pump for the turndown condition.

\section{Design Basis and Staging Strategy}

The 150\,kW cooling duty is split across \textbf{two identical 75\,kW parallel
refrigerant circuits}, each with its own compressor, evaporator/condenser water and
glycol connections, and EEV. This chapter covers the compressor selection and
performance modeling common to both circuits; the two units are assumed identical.

The staging rationale (developed alongside this selection):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Redundancy}: losing one compressor leaves 50\,\% capacity available,
        versus 25\,\% for an unequal split --- a materially better partial-failure
        state for a data-center cooling system.
  \item \textbf{IPLV coverage}: 50\,\% and 100\,\% load points are met by exactly one
        and two units running at their own full capacity, respectively; the 25\,\% and
        75\,\% points require partial loading of the active unit(s), discussed in
        \S\ref{comp:sec:staging}.
  \item \textbf{Parts commonality}: one compressor model, one spare-parts inventory,
        one service procedure.
\end{itemize}

% ── §9.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{AHRI Standard 540 / EN12900 Ten-Coefficient Performance Map}
\label{comp:sec:map}

Rather than a fixed isentropic/volumetric efficiency pair, compressor performance is
modeled directly from the manufacturer's published polynomial map, in the standard
AHRI-540/EN12900 form:

\[
  y = c_1 + c_2\,t_o + c_3\,t_c + c_4\,t_o^2 + c_5\,t_o t_c + c_6\,t_c^2
      + c_7\,t_o^3 + c_8\,t_c t_o^2 + c_9\,t_o t_c^2 + c_{10}\,t_c^3
\]

with $t_o$, $t_c$ the evaporating and condensing \emph{dew points} in \textdegree C, and
$y$ evaluated separately for cooling capacity $Q$ [W], power input $P$ [W], and mass
flow $\mdot$ [kg/h] using three independent coefficient sets (Table~\ref{tab:ahricoeff}).
This directly resolves the constant-efficiency limitation of the earlier compressor
model --- efficiency now varies correctly with pressure ratio (i.e. with $t_o$ and
$t_c$) since it is built into the polynomial's own curvature, rather than assumed
constant.

\caption{AHRI-540 coefficients, Bitzer 4FEP-35Z, R-290.}
\label{tab:ahricoeff}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lLLL}
\toprule
& \textbf{$Q$ [W]} & \textbf{$P$ [W]} & \textbf{$\mdot$ [kg/h]} \\
\midrule
$c_1$  & 114372.76 & 7147.24  & 1009.99 \\
$c_2$  & 3903.60   & $-$239.01 & 36.10 \\
$c_3$  & $-$1121.94 & 255.72  & $-$4.34 \\
$c_4$  & 45.37     & $-$7.87  & 0.494 \\
$c_5$  & $-$33.97  & 8.65     & $-$0.0997 \\
$c_6$  & 3.40      & 1.62     & 0.0378 \\
$c_7$  & 0.168     & $-$0.0412 & 0.00287 \\
$c_8$  & $-$0.338  & 0.0698   & $-$0.00109 \\
$c_9$  & 0.00990   & 0.01026  & $-$0.0000239 \\
$c_{10}$ & $-$0.0256 & $-$0.0204 & $-$0.000382 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\textbf{Verification:} evaluating the map at the design point ($t_o=7$, $t_c=50$)
reproduces the vendor's stated rated performance exactly (80.65\,kW / 22.74\,kW /
1079.7\,kg/h, COP 3.55) --- confirming the digitized/transcribed coefficients are
correctly implemented.

% ── §9.4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Rating-Basis Correction: EN12900 vs.\ Actual Application}
\label{comp:sec:correction}

The map coefficients reproduce performance at the \textbf{EN12900 standard rating
basis}: a suction (return) gas temperature fixed at 20\,\textdegree C \emph{absolute}
(not a fixed superheat --- the implied superheat therefore changes with $t_o$), and 0\,K
liquid subcooling. This is a testing/rating convention, not the actual application
operating point (8\,K useful superheat, 15\,\textdegree C actual return gas). A
density-ratio correction is applied:

\[
  \mathrm{DR} = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{actual}}(T_{\mathrm{suction,actual}}, p_o)}
                     {\rho_{\mathrm{rated}}(20\,\text{\textdegree C}, p_o)}
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item Mass flow scales directly with DR (same physical displacement and volumetric
        efficiency --- a denser suction gas simply packs more mass per stroke).
  \item Capacity is recomputed directly from the corrected mass flow and the
        \emph{actual} refrigeration effect ($h_{\mathrm{suction,actual}} -
        h_{\mathrm{liquid,actual}}$), which also captures the (smaller) enthalpy shift
        from the different suction temperature --- more rigorous than scaling capacity
        by DR alone.
  \item Power input is scaled by the same DR (standard simplified industry practice;
        specific compression work is only weakly sensitive to suction superheat over
        this range).
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[h!]
\caption{Design-point performance: catalog (EN12900) vs.\ corrected (actual application).}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lLL}
\toprule
& \textbf{Catalog (EN12900)} & \textbf{Corrected (8\,K superheat)} \\
\midrule
Cooling capacity & 80.65\,kW & 80.00\,kW \\
Power input & 22.74\,kW & 23.32\,kW \\
Mass flow & 1079.7\,kg/h & 1107.1\,kg/h \\
COP & 3.55 & 3.43 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The correction is small at the design point ($\mathrm{DR}=1.025$) but is not
negligible --- COP drops from 3.55 to 3.43, which is worth carrying through rather than
using the uncorrected catalog numbers directly, especially since the correction grows
at operating points further from the rating condition (e.g.\ colder $t_o$, where the
20\,\textdegree C fixed return-gas rating implies a much larger rated superheat than
the actual 8\,K).

\zonedivider{Part B \quad---\quad Two-Compressor Bank: Staging and Evaporating-Temperature Solve}

% ── §9.5 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Load-Following Logic}
\label{comp:sec:staging}

Both compressors sit on a common suction/discharge manifold and therefore always share
the same $t_o$/$t_c$ --- capacity is not set directly, but emerges from where the
active compressor(s)' map intersects the required duty at the prevailing condensing
temperature, i.e.\ the evaporating temperature ``floats'' until
$n_{\mathrm{active}} \times Q_{\mathrm{unit}}(t_o, t_c) = Q_{\mathrm{required}}$. This
is solved numerically (bisection, bounded to $t_o \in [-10, 15]\,$\textdegree C) for a
given required duty and condensing temperature, with the smallest $n_{\mathrm{active}}$
that can reach the load selected first (better part-load efficiency, since each unit
stays closer to its own best-efficiency region rather than being needlessly staged
alongside another partially-loaded unit).

% ── §9.6 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Finding: Low-Load Turndown Floor}
\label{comp:sec:floor}

The 25\,\% (37.5\,kW) and minimum-IT-load (30\,kW) points do \emph{not} solve --- even
a single compressor at the coldest evaporating temperature allowed
($t_o=-10\,$\textdegree C) still delivers $\approx$42\,kW, above both targets. This is
a genuine physical limit of this compressor selection, not a solver artifact: a
fixed-speed, non-unloading reciprocating compressor has a bounded capacity range as a
function of $t_o$ alone, and this unit's floor sits above the project's minimum load.

This is a materially important finding for the project and needs to be resolved by one
of:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cylinder unloading} on the 4FEP-35Z (if available for this model) to
        step capacity down discretely at fixed $t_o$;
  \item \textbf{A VFD} on one compressor, trading the fixed-speed map for a
        speed-dependent one;
  \item \textbf{Hot-gas bypass}, at an efficiency penalty (already visible in the COP
        collapse toward the floor: 2.02 vs.\ 3.4+ at design conditions);
  \item \textbf{A smaller trim/pony compressor} sized specifically for the 20--30\,\%
        load range;
  \item Or relying on the \textbf{economizer} (Chapter~\ref{ch:econ}) to cover low-load
        hours instead of the compressor bank entirely, if those hours reliably
        coincide with sufficiently low ambient temperatures --- this needs the annual
        simulation to confirm, since low IT load and low ambient are not
        guaranteed to coincide.
\end{itemize}

\section{Purpose and Design Basis}
\label{oilsep:sec:basis}

some fraction of that oil leaves the compressor entrained in the discharge vapour. An
oil separator is installed immediately downstream of the discharge manifold to recover

evaporator heat-transfer surfaces and protecting the compressor from running oil-lean.

discharge manifold, so a single separator sized for the combined discharge flow is used

% ── §9b.3 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Primary-Only: No Secondary Separator Required}
\label{oilsep:sec:primaryonly}

Bitzer's secondary/fine-filter oil separators (OAS series) and combined units (OAC
series) exist primarily to serve \textbf{NH\textsubscript{3} (R-717)} systems: ammonia is
effectively immiscible with the mineral/synthetic oils used in ammonia compressors, so a
single primary separator cannot reliably strip the last traces of oil mist, and a
secondary fine-filter stage is standard practice.

R-290 is the opposite case: propane is \textbf{highly miscible} with the polyolester oil
used in the 4FEP-35Z (Chapter~\ref{ch:comp}), which is itself a key reason hydrocarbon
refrigerant/oil systems achieve good primary separation efficiency ($\geq$99\,\% typical)
without a secondary stage. Accordingly, only the primary OA1954 unit is carried in the
BOM; no OAS/OAC secondary or combined separator is included.

% ── §9b.4 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Oil Return and A3 Ignition-Source Avoidance}
\label{oilsep:sec:oilreturn}

The oil return line from the separator back to the compressor crankcase should use a
\textbf{float-actuated mechanical return} rather than an electrically energized solenoid
valve. For an A3-classified refrigerant, this avoids introducing an electrical
ignition source directly in the refrigerant-wetted oil-return path; if an electric
solenoid oil-return valve is used elsewhere in the design, it must carry ATEX (or
equivalent) explosion-protection certification. This is carried forward as a design
constraint for the P\&ID and control-interaction discussion (still outstanding per the
project's overall open-items list).

The separator's oil heater (1\,$\times$\,140\,W, \S\ref{oilsep:sec:selected}) must remain
energized whenever the compressors are at standstill, consistent with Bitzer's general
guidance for hydrocarbon refrigerants: insufficient oil temperature during shut-off
allows excess refrigerant to dissolve into the oil, reducing viscosity at the next
start and increasing oil carry-over.

\section{Scope and Method}
\label{piping:sec:scope}

The project brief scopes piping to \textbf{diameters only --- no lengths, fittings, or
supports} (subtask 1d), and separately instructs that pressure losses within the piping
are to be neglected. This chapter closes that deliverable for all three fluid circuits in
the system:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The \textbf{R-290 refrigerant circuit} --- suction, discharge (hot-gas), and
        liquid lines connecting the compressor, condenser, and evaporator
        (Chapters~\ref{ch:comp}, \ref{ch:cond}, \ref{ch:evap}).
  \item The \textbf{glycol loop} --- already partially sized in
        Chapter~\ref{ch:pump} (\S\ref{pump:sec:piping}); restated and cross-checked here.
  \item The \textbf{chilled-water loop} --- sized here for the first time.
\end{enumerate}

For the refrigerant circuit, sizing follows the project's \textbf{Table 1} streaming
velocities, reproduced below, which vary by refrigerant \emph{state} rather than by loop:

\begin{table}[h!]
\caption{Streaming velocities within charge lines (project brief, Table 1).}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lL}
\toprule
\textbf{State} & \textbf{Velocity, $v$} \\
\midrule
Gaseous --- low pressure  & 4.5--20\,m/s \\
Gaseous --- high pressure & 10--18\,m/s \\
Liquid                    & $<1.5$\,m/s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent No equivalent table exists for the glycol or chilled-water loops, so those two
circuits instead follow the conventional closed-loop hydronic velocity guideline
(1--2.4\,m/s) already adopted in Chapters~\ref{ch:pump} and \ref{ch:chwpump}, for
consistency with those chapters' pump selections.

\textbf{Common method for all six lines below:}

\begin{equation}
  D_{\mathrm{req}} = \sqrt{\frac{4\,\mdot}{\pi\,\rho\,v_{\mathrm{design}}}}
  \tag{Eq.\,P.1}
\end{equation}

\noindent A design velocity $v_{\mathrm{design}}$ is chosen inside the applicable band,
$D_{\mathrm{req}}$ is computed from the design mass flow and fluid density at that state,
and the result is \textbf{rounded up} to the nearest standard tube/pipe size (ACR copper
for refrigerant lines, Schedule 40 steel for the glycol/CHW loops, matching
Chapter~\ref{ch:pump}'s choice of material). The actual velocity at the selected standard
size is then rechecked against the band.

% ── §11.1 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Purpose and Design Basis}
\label{receiver:sec:basis}

The liquid receiver sits on the high-pressure side of the circuit, between the condenser
outlet (Chapter~\ref{ch:cond}) and the EEV inlet (Chapter~\ref{ch:eev}). Its function is
to buffer the refrigerant inventory as it migrates around the circuit, and to guarantee
that the EEV always sees fully subcooled liquid rather than a liquid/vapour mixture.

This buffering role is not incidental for this project --- the control strategy switches
between three distinct operating modes based on outdoor air temperature (Chapter~\ref{ch:dry},
\S\ref{dry:sec:annualregime}), and each mode redistributes refrigerant differently around the
circuit:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Full mechanical} ($>10\,$\textdegree C OAT): the compressor runs
        continuously, liquid inventory stacks up through the condenser and receiver.
        Occurs $\approx$55.5\,\% of the year (TMY3, Champaign, IL).
  \item \textbf{Partial free cooling} (4--10\,\textdegree C OAT): mixed operation.
        Occurs $\approx$15.5\,\% of the year.
  \item \textbf{Full free cooling} ($\leq$4\,\textdegree C OAT): the compressor is off and
        the economiser (Chapter~\ref{ch:econ}) carries the full load; refrigerant
        migrates toward the low-pressure side and sits largely static. Occurs
        $\approx$29\,\% of the year.
\end{itemize}

A receiver undersized for this migration risks flash gas at the EEV inlet (eroding the
5\,K design subcooling from Chapter~\ref{ch:cond}, \S\ref{cond:sec:subcool}), backed-up
liquid inside the condenser (reducing active condensing area and raising head pressure),
and --- in the worst case --- liquid reaching the vapour-equalisation line back to the
condenser rather than staying clear of it.

% ── §11.2 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{System Refrigerant Charge Estimate (Order-of-Magnitude)}
\label{receiver:sec:charge}

No dedicated EN~378 charge calculation exists yet for this system (it remains an open
item across the report, most recently flagged in Chapter~\ref{ch:oilsep}). To size the
receiver, a preliminary order-of-magnitude estimate is built here directly from the
refrigerant-side geometry already assigned in \texttt{evaporator.py} and
\texttt{condenser.py}, plus the line sizes selected in Chapter~\ref{ch:piping}. This
estimate should be treated as \textbf{indicative, not final} --- see
\S\ref{receiver:sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Heat Exchanger Refrigerant-Side Holdup}

Both BPHEs share the same assigned channel geometry ($D_h = 4\,$mm, $\phi = 1.17$,
$N_{cp} = 38$, $L_w = 0.2\,$m, $L = 0.5\,$m), giving an identical refrigerant-side
channel volume for each unit:

\[
  b = \frac{D_h\,\phi}{2} = 2.34\,\mathrm{mm}, \qquad
  V_{\mathrm{channel}} = N_{cp}\,b\,L_w\,L = 8.89\;\mathrm{L}
\]

Because both exchangers operate two-phase over most of their length, neither is fully
liquid nor fully vapour internally. CoolProp-evaluated bounding densities at each unit's
operating condition are used to bracket the holdup, then a representative mid-range
value is adopted:

\subsection{Liquid Line Holdup}

The liquid line (Chapter~\ref{ch:piping}, \S\ref{piping:sec:liquid}) is 1-5/8\,in ACR
copper, 38.23\,mm actual ID, carrying subcooled liquid at 47\,\textdegree C
($\rho = 454.7\,$kg/m$^3$, CoolProp). \textbf{No line length is specified by the project
brief} (piping scope is diameters-only, \S\ref{piping:sec:scope}); a representative
15\,m run is \assumed{} here purely for charge-estimation purposes, following the same
type of documented length assumption already used for pump duty-point calculations
(Chapter~\ref{ch:pump}, \S\ref{pump:sec:piping}, 65\,m glycol run).

\[
  V_{\mathrm{line}} = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.03823)^2 \times 15\;\mathrm{m} = 17.2\;\mathrm{L}
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  m_{\mathrm{line}} = V_{\mathrm{line}} \times 454.7\;\mathrm{kg/m^3} = 7.8\;\mathrm{kg}
\]

This single term is the largest contributor to the estimate, and is also the most
uncertain --- it scales directly with an assumed length that has no basis yet in an
actual equipment layout.

\subsection{Suction and Discharge Line Holdup}

Both lines carry low-density superheated vapour: $\approx$12.7\,kg/m$^3$ at the suction
condition and 34.9\,kg/m$^3$ at the discharge condition (72\,\textdegree C,
17.89\,bar(a), CoolProp) --- one to two orders of magnitude below the liquid-side
densities above. Even accounting for the suction line's larger 62.87\,mm ID
(Chapter~\ref{ch:piping}, Table~\ref{tab:liquid_candidates}), their combined holdup is
well under 1\,kg for any plausible run length and is neglected below.

% ── §11.3 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Design Pressure Determination}
\label{receiver:sec:pressure}

As with the evaporator and condenser BPHEs (\S\ref{ch:evap}, design pressure section),
the receiver's design pressure is governed by the \textbf{standstill condition}, not the
running condensing pressure. With the system off and no flow, refrigerant equalises to
the highest ambient the unit may see; using the same 55\,\textdegree C soak-temperature
basis (ASHRAE~15 / EN~378 / IEC~60335-2-40) already adopted elsewhere in this report:

\[
  p_{\mathrm{sat}}(55\,\text{\textdegree C}) = 19.07\;\mathrm{bar(a)} \quad\text{(CoolProp)}
\]

This matches the 19.1\,bar(a) figure independently derived in \S\ref{ch:evap}, confirming
consistency across chapters. The receiver's design pressure must exceed this value with
margin.

\subsection{Horizontal vs.\ Vertical Orientation}
\label{receiver:sec:orientation}

Horizontal (F-series) is selected over vertical (FS-series). A horizontal vessel spreads
liquid over a much larger surface area for a given internal volume, so the liquid level
changes only modestly as charge redistributes between modes; a vertical vessel of equal
volume sees a proportionally larger level swing for the same charge shift, since the
liquid pools in a narrower cross-section. Given this system's mode-switching behaviour
(\S\ref{receiver:sec:basis}) --- charge actively migrating around the loop roughly 45\,\%
of the year (partial + full free cooling combined) --- the larger safety margin against
uncovering the liquid outlet or flooding the vapour-equalisation line favours horizontal.
The tradeoff is footprint: horizontal units occupy more floor area than a vertical unit
of equal volume, judged acceptable here given no stated floor-space constraint for the
mechanical room.

% ── §11.6 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{A3 Safety Considerations}
\label{receiver:sec:safety}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Charge minimisation.} Per IEC~60335-2-40 and EN~378, allowable A3
        refrigerant charge scales with occupied-space ventilation and installation
        category. Every additional litre of receiver volume raises total system charge
        and pushes the installation toward stricter ventilation/leak-detection
        requirements --- the driving reason for selecting the smallest model that clears
        the charge estimate (\S\ref{receiver:sec:selected}) rather than adding margin for
        its own sake.
  \item \textbf{Relief discharge routing.} Bitzer's own documentation for this series
        states explicitly that when the pressure relief valve opens, flammable and
        explosive refrigerant is released from the system; the relief discharge must be
        routed outdoors to a safe location, away from ignition sources, per EN~378 ---
        not vented indoors into the mechanical room.
  \item \textbf{Consistency with the oil separator chapter.} The same 55\,\textdegree C
        standstill-pressure basis and A3 handling requirements applied to the oil
        separator selection (Chapter~\ref{ch:oilsep}, \S\ref{oilsep:sec:basis}) are
        applied here, keeping the two pressure-vessel selections on a consistent basis.
\end{itemize}

% ── §11.7 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Limitations and Open Items}
\label{receiver:sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The system charge estimate (\S\ref{receiver:sec:charge}) is
        order-of-magnitude, not a completed EN~378 charge calculation.} It is not yet
        cross-checked against a full charge calc, which remains an open item across the
        report (Chapter~\ref{ch:oilsep}, \S\ref{oilsep:sec:limitations}).
  \item \textbf{The dominant term (liquid line, $\approx$7.8\,kg) rests on an
        \assumed{} 15\,m line length}, chosen for estimation purposes only since the
        project brief scopes piping to diameters, not lengths
        (\S\ref{piping:sec:scope}). The total estimate scales close to linearly with this
        assumption --- a 10\,m run gives $\approx$8.0\,kg total; a 20\,m run gives
        $\approx$13.1\,kg total. \textbf{This should be revisited once an actual
        equipment layout is available.}
  \item \textbf{Selected model leaves very little charge margin.} F252HP's 11.3\,kg
        rated maximum sits within $\approx$10\,\% of the $\approx$10.5\,kg point estimate
        --- i.e., essentially no margin once the estimate's own $\approx$9--13\,kg range
        is considered. If the liquid-line length assumption above lands anywhere past
        $\approx$16\,m, or if the pending EN~378 calculation finds additional charge
        elsewhere in the circuit, \textbf{F302NP (13.5\,kg) or F392TP/F402HP (17.6\,kg)
        should be adopted instead.} This is a deliberate, flagged risk of the current
        selection, not an oversight.
  \item \textbf{Connection size vs.\ liquid line: a real mismatch, not yet resolved.}
        F252HP's 22\,mm (7/8\,in) connections are markedly smaller than the 38.23\,mm
        (1-5/8\,in) liquid line selected in Chapter~\ref{ch:piping}. Checking velocity
        through the receiver's own connection at the design mass flow
        ($\mdot_r = 0.553\,$kg/s, $\rho = 454.7\,$kg/m$^3$ at 47\,\textdegree C):
        \[
          v_{\mathrm{conn}} = \frac{\mdot_r}{\rho \times \frac{\pi}{4}(0.022)^2}
          \approx 3.2\;\mathrm{m/s}
        \]
        This is more than double the project brief's 1.5\,m/s liquid-line ceiling
        (Chapter~\ref{ch:piping}, Table~1). \textbf{A reducer/adapter fitting at the
        receiver connections does not resolve this} --- it is the connection itself, not
        just the transition, that is undersized relative to the selected liquid line.
        This is the report's most significant open item on this chapter and should be
        resolved before procurement, most likely by moving to a model with 28\,mm
        (1\,1/8\,in) or larger connections (F392TP/F402HP, or larger), which is also
        consistent with item 3 above.
  \item \textbf{Selection basis inherits the same $t_c$ mismatch flagged elsewhere.} The
        47\,\textdegree C liquid-outlet condition used throughout this chapter follows the
        condenser chapter's 52\,\textdegree C / 5\,K subcooling basis
        (\S\ref{cond:sec:subcool}), not the compressor chapter's 50\,\textdegree C / 0\,K
        basis (Chapter~\ref{ch:comp}, \S\ref{comp:sec:limitations}). Once that
        cross-chapter discrepancy is resolved, the density values in
        \S\ref{receiver:sec:charge} should be re-evaluated.
  \item \textbf{Final model should be confirmed in Bitzer's selection software} with
        R-290 entered directly, the same practice already recommended for the oil
        separator (Chapter~\ref{ch:oilsep}, \S\ref{oilsep:sec:limitations}), before this
        model is locked into the BOM.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Method}
\label{perf:sec:scope}

This chapter reports the three standardized efficiency metrics the project brief
requires (subtask~1f): the \textbf{full-load COP}, the \textbf{Integrated Part Load
Value (IPLV.IP)}, and the \textbf{design-day facility PUE} --- followed by the
weather-driven \textbf{seasonal performance} across the real Champaign TMY3 year.

\subsection{Design Revision for Efficiency Compliance}
\label{perf:sec:revision}

The design as originally sized (component Chapters~\ref{ch:evap}--\ref{ch:receiver})
\textbf{missed both COP-based targets}: full-load COP 2.64 at the AHRI rating point
(target $\geq3.5$) and IPLV.IP 4.43 (target $\geq5.0$). An option study on the
compressor map identified the minimal set of changes that reaches compliance without
altering the architecture, the refrigerant, or the compressor model; this chapter's
reported metrics are for the \textbf{revised design}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Condensing approach reduced from 17\,K to 10\,K}: dry cooler approach
        5\,$\to$\,3\,K, glycol rise 6\,$\to$\,4\,K, condenser approach 6\,$\to$\,3\,K,
        giving $t_c = 45$\,\textdegree C (was 52\,\textdegree C) at the
        35\,\textdegree C design ambient. Cost: more heat-transfer area
        (\S\ref{perf:sec:hardware}).
  \item \textbf{Evaporator approach reduced from 8\,K to 5\,K}: $t_o = 10$\,\textdegree C
        (was 7\,\textdegree C) at the 15\,\textdegree C design LCHW. Cost: more
        evaporator plates.
  \item \textbf{VFDs on the compressors}: at full load the two-unit bank over-delivers
        at the evaporator-dictated $t_o$ ($\approx$201\,kW available at
        $t_o=10$/$t_c=45$\,\textdegree C), so the drive trims speed ($\approx$75\,\%) to
        deliver exactly 150\,kW there --- instead of letting $t_o$ float down as a
        fixed-speed bank must; and below the old turndown floor it \emph{modulates
        capacity} instead of hot-gas bypassing, removing the Point-D COP collapse
        (\S\ref{perf:sec:iplv}). COP is taken as speed-invariant (capacity and power
        both scale $\approx$linearly with speed for a reciprocating compressor), a
        neutral-to-conservative standard simplification.
\end{itemize}

The component chapters document the \emph{original} sizing basis; the hardware
consequences of this revision (larger dry cooler, more HX plates, higher glycol flow)
are quantified in \S\ref{perf:sec:hardware}. All compressor performance is drawn from
the same Bitzer 4FEP-35Z AHRI-540 map used throughout the report
(Chapter~\ref{ch:comp}); the VFD operating model is
\texttt{compressor.solve\_with\_vfd()}, and the metrics are implemented in
\texttt{performance.py}.

\section{Full-Load COP}
\label{perf:sec:fullload}

chilled-water temperature, which sets $t_o$ via the revised 5\,K evaporator approach.

% ── §12.3 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Integrated Part Load Value (IPLV.IP)}
\label{perf:sec:iplv}

IPLV.IP is the AHRI 550/590-2023~\S5.2 weighted part-load COP,
$0.01A + 0.42B + 0.45C + 0.12D$, evaluated at 100/75/50/25\,\% load with each point at
its own reduced condenser-entering-air temperature (35/26.7/18.3/12.8\,\textdegree C).
Each air temperature is mapped to a condensing temperature via the revised 10\,K
approach, and the compressor bank is staged (Chapter~\ref{ch:comp}) to deliver the
reduced load, with the VFD modulating capacity below the fixed-speed turndown floor.

\input{tables/performance/iplv.tex}

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/performance/design_point/iplv_points.png}
\caption{IPLV.IP part-load points (revised design). Point~D (25\,\% load) runs on VFD
speed modulation below the fixed-speed turndown floor.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Result: IPLV.IP $= 5.23$, above the $\geq5.0$ target.} Relative to the original
design's 4.43, the gain comes from two revision elements acting together:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The 10\,K condensing approach} lowers $t_c$ at every point (e.g.\
        Point~C condenses at 28.3 instead of 35.3\,\textdegree C), lifting the two
        heavily weighted mid-load points: B reaches COP 6.18 (weight 0.42) and C
        reaches 4.62 (weight 0.45).
  \item \textbf{VFD capacity modulation replaces hot-gas bypass at Point~D}: instead
        of running at the floor and throwing away capacity as recirculated hot gas
        (original COP 2.29), the drive slows the compressor so power scales down with
        the delivered duty, holding COP at the floor operating point's map value ---
        4.35. This is deliberately \emph{conservative}: in reality, reduced speed lets
        the evaporating temperature float upward (the evaporator is 4$\times$
        oversized at 25\,\% load), which would raise the point's COP further.
\end{itemize}

The turndown-floor open item flagged in Chapter~\ref{ch:comp}
(\S\ref{comp:sec:floor}) is thereby resolved by the VFD option that chapter itself
listed as a candidate strategy --- and the same drive also covers the 30\,kW
minimum-IT-load condition without bypass.

% ── §12.4 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Design-Day PUE}
\label{perf:sec:pue}

Facility PUE is (IT load $+$ other facility loads)$/$IT load on a design-day basis, with
the IT load taken as numerically equal to the 150\,kW design cooling duty (the project's
near-constant-load assumption). ``Other facility loads'' are the mechanical-mode
electrical loads at the 35\,\textdegree C / full-load design point.

\input{tables/performance/design_day_pue.tex}

The design-day PUE is \textbf{1.31} (improved from the original design's 1.40),
dominated by the compressor bank (31.7\,kW of the 45.9\,kW total --- itself down from
47.1\,kW, the revision's lift reduction at work). The glycol pump load roughly doubles
(1.18\,$\to$\,2.30\,kW) because the tighter 4\,K glycol range demands
$\approx$28\,\% more flow --- a real, counted cost of the revision, far outweighed by
the 15.4\,kW compressor saving. This is the \emph{worst-case} PUE --- it occurs only
at the hottest design ambient; the seasonal analysis below shows the annual-average
PUE is far more favourable because most of the year runs at part load or in free
cooling.

% ── §12.7 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Hardware Consequences of the Revision}
\label{perf:sec:hardware}

The compliance gains are bought with heat-transfer area and a drive --- quantified
here so the revision's cost is as explicit as its benefit.

\subsection{Revised Overall Heat-Transfer Coefficients}
\label{perf:sec:uvalues}

The tighter approaches raise the required $UA$ of both plate exchangers
(evaporator 14.0\,$\to$\,19.7\,kW/K; condenser 13.1\,$\to$\,20.2\,kW/K). That extra
$UA$ can be bought either as \emph{area} (more plates) or as \emph{coefficient} (a
higher $U$). The original chapters deliberately assumed values at the conservative
end of their published bands ($U_{\mathrm{evap}} = 3{,}000$,
$U_{\mathrm{cond}} = 2{,}500$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$); the revision re-assumes them
near the top of those bands, which keeps both exchangers inside the same
GBH-DW\,500H frame:

\begin{table}[h!]
\caption{Revised overall heat-transfer coefficient assumptions.}
\label{tab:revision_u}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lLLLL}
\toprule
\textbf{Exchanger} & \textbf{Published band} & \textbf{Original} & \textbf{Revised} & \textbf{Justification} \\
\midrule
Evaporator (water\,/\,R-290) & 2{,}500--4{,}500 & 3{,}000 & \textbf{4{,}000} &
Near the top of the band, consistent with propane's favourable transport properties
already noted in \S2.2.1 \\
Condenser (R-290\,/\,30\,\% PG) & 2{,}000--3{,}500 & 2{,}500 & \textbf{3{,}650} &
Marginally \emph{above} the band; the small excess is attributed to the revision's
$+28$\,\% glycol flow (\S\ref{perf:sec:hardware}) raising the glycol-side film
coefficient, the very term \S3.2.4 identifies as the limiting one \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent \textbf{These are assumptions, not results}, and they are the least
certain input in the revision. $U$ is an \emph{outcome} of real plate geometry and
channel velocity, not a free parameter, and the condenser value sits just outside
its published band. Both must be confirmed in \textbf{Kelvion Select PHE} before
procurement; if the vendor returns materially lower coefficients, the plate counts
below rise and the 500H frame limit returns as a binding constraint. The same
selection run should also report the resulting channel pressure drops, since a
higher $U$ is bought with higher channel velocity and therefore feeds back into
pump sizing (Chapters~\ref{ch:pump}, \ref{ch:chwpump}).

\noindent None of these changes the architecture, the refrigerant, or the component
